# 3795. Will India at Least Now Understand Sri Lanka?

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil is reproduced in full below the translation and remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.” Literally evoking “one born alongside me,” Kalaignar uses it as a distinctive address of shared identity, equality, affection, and solidarity within the movement.

**Udanpirappē,**

You would have read the statement I issued yesterday insisting that India should not participate in the Commonwealth conference to be held in Sri Lanka. In it, I said that both before and after Sri Lanka attained independence—and during the periods of Pandit Nehru, Mother Indira Gandhi and the young leader Rajiv Gandhi—Sri Lanka had failed to honour every agreement made for the Eelam Tamils, violated those agreements and completely forfeited its credibility in the eyes of the world. This letter is intended to recall the historical events behind that statement.

From 1920 onwards, Malayalis began travelling from India to Colombo and taking up various kinds of work. Their number, about 1,000 in 1920, rose to around 30,000 by 1930. In 1935, Sinhalese voices were raised against them. A.E. Goonesinha claimed that Malayalis were taking away employment from the Sinhalese. When Communist leader A.K. Gopalan visited Colombo in 1939 and tried to build links among the Left, Sinhalese disrupted his address at a textile mill in Wellawatte on 1 May 1939. As a result of such developments, Malayalis began leaving Colombo during the 1940s.

In that same year, 1939, despite fierce opposition from Tamil members, the Ceylon State Council adopted A.E. Goonesinha’s proposal that 15,000 Tamils be sent back to India. It also adopted, again despite strong Tamil opposition, D.S. Senanayake’s proposal to remove Tamils from Tamil Nadu who held government employment.

On 26 July 1939, Sinhalese led by A.E. Goonesinha entered the public meeting at Galle Face Green in Colombo where Nehru was speaking and raised counter-slogans to create disorder. Nehru was not frightened by the commotion. He came down from the platform and walked towards the hooligans trying to disrupt the gathering. Yet the disturbance made it impossible for him to continue his speech.

Consider also what happened in 1987. Before Rajiv Gandhi departed after signing the Rajiv–Jayewardene Accord, a Sinhala naval rating struck him with the butt of his rifle during the guard of honour at the Presidential Palace. Rajiv escaped without injury. Only two months ago, the same sailor proudly said in an interview that he did not regret attacking Rajiv Gandhi. Does this not reveal how deeply hostility towards Indians has taken root in Sinhala minds?

Beginning in 2000, Norway took the lead, with countries including the United States, Japan and India also making efforts. As a result, in 2002 a memorandum of understanding and a ceasefire agreement were reached between the Sri Lankan Government and the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam.

On 8 January 2008, the Sri Lankan Government unilaterally terminated that agreement. The Sinhala Government expelled both the Norwegian mediator and the ceasefire-monitoring mission from Sri Lanka.

Giving assurances to Tamils, signing agreements with them, later withdrawing from those agreements and violating them without respect has become a traditional habit of the Sinhalese. It has not only worsened relations between Sinhalese and Tamils; it has made goodwill and reconciliation between the two peoples virtually impossible.

From as early as 1925, the Sinhalese have possessed a history of treachery in trampling upon and violating each agreement reached with the Tamils. Let me briefly set out that record.

On 28 June 1925, Vaitilingam Duraiswamy, representing the Ceylon Tamil Mahajana Sabha, and C.E. Corea, representing the executive committee of the Ceylon National Congress, signed the “Mahendra Pact” in Jaffna. The Sinhalese unilaterally broke it.

Contrary to Section 29 of the Constitution granted by the British for the protection of minorities, the Ceylon Citizenship Act of 1948 and the Indian and Pakistani Residents Citizenship Act of 1949 deprived ten lakh Hill Country Tamils of citizenship.

In the 1930s, it was Jayewardene who spoke in the Ceylon Parliament in favour of both Sinhala and Tamil being official languages. In 1954, Prime Minister John Kotelawala vowed on the grounds of Kokkuvil Hindu College that he would make both languages official. Yet the United National Party to which both belonged went against those assurances and resolved at its 1955 convention in Kelaniya that “Sinhala alone” would be the official language.

Again contrary to the minority protection contained in Section 29 of the Constitution, Prime Minister Bandaranaike of the Sri Lanka Freedom Party secured passage of the “Sinhala Only” Act in Parliament on 5 June 1956.

On 26 July, Bandaranaike entered into an agreement with S.J.V. Chelvanayakam to establish regional councils. Unable to withstand pressure from extreme Sinhala nationalists, the same Prime Minister cancelled it in May 1958.

Without seeking the wishes of five lakh Hill Country Tamils, Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike disregarded the 1964 Sirima–Shastri Pact and deported Hill Country Tamils to India.

On 25 March 1965, Prime Minister Dudley Senanayake of the United National Party reached a power-sharing agreement with Chelvanayakam. Frightened by protest marches organised by extreme Sinhala nationalists—in one of which a Buddhist monk was killed on 8 January 1966—he cancelled that agreement.

Without Tamil participation and without British approval, Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike framed the Constitution of 22 May 1972, unilaterally declaring Sri Lanka a republic. Colvin R. de Silva, who had spoken as a socialist in Parliament in 1956 of “one language, two nations; two languages, one nation,” later became a Sinhala chauvinist and drafted a Constitution that placed Buddhism foremost, made Sinhala the official language and prevented Tamils from sharing in governance.

The 1978 Constitution, which introduced an executive presidency possessing governmental power, was framed by Prime Minister J.R. Jayewardene. Persons close to the Sinhala Government burned the Jaffna Public Library—that treasury of knowledge—in 1981. In 1983, the Government itself planned and carried out ethnic violence, forcing many thousands of Tamils to flee the country.

From 3 January 1984 onwards, the envoy of Indian Prime Minister Indira Gandhi visited Colombo several times and prepared proposals after consultations with both the Government and the Tamil side. President J.R. Jayewardene sent them to an all-party conference, then completely ignored the conference’s recommendations and, on 26 August 1984, defeated Prime Minister Indira Gandhi’s peace initiative.

The Thimphu talks convened by Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi failed on 17 August 1985 because the Sri Lankan Government lacked any willingness to compromise.

The all-party conference in Colombo on 25 June 1986 and the Rajiv–Jayewardene summit in Bengaluru on 18 November 1986 likewise failed because of the Sri Lankan Government’s obstinate policy.

The Rajiv–Jayewardene Accord was signed in Colombo in July 1987. It accepted that Sri Lanka’s Northern and Eastern Provinces were the traditional homeland of Tamil-speaking people. It also accepted that the two provinces would be joined as a single administrative unit under one Provincial Council, one Governor, one Chief Minister and one Council of Ministers.

As a consequence of that Accord, the Thirteenth Amendment to Sri Lanka’s Constitution, devolving powers to the Provincial Councils, came into existence on 14 November 1987. Efforts are now being intensified to dilute that Amendment and create a provincial administration without real governing powers.

The events I have listed make clear Sri Lanka’s untrustworthy practice of unilaterally destroying every agreement it has entered into with the Tamils and with the Government of India.

There is no need to imagine that we are deliberately making allegations against President Rajapaksa with some hidden motive, or that without any foundation we seek to place him before the world as a war criminal responsible for the massacre of Tamils and demand an international inquiry. Listen to what Lee Kuan Yew—the first Prime Minister of Singapore, acclaimed as the architect of modern Singapore and father of its present Prime Minister—said in an interview about Sri Lanka’s present condition:

> “Tamils have been in Sri Lanka for as long as the Sinhalese have. There is no environment in which Tamils and Sinhalese can live together. So long as Sri Lanka remains a unitary state, it cannot be a happy country. President Rajapaksa says that the Liberation Tigers, who fought for the Tamils, have been defeated and that Sri Lanka’s ethnic problem has therefore been solved. He expects others to believe this. But the Tamils will not remain submissive. Nor will they run away in fear of the Sinhalese. I have read President Rajapaksa’s speeches. He is a Sinhala extremist; I know this well. His mind cannot be changed and he cannot be reformed. Sri Lanka is not happy today. The Sinhalese certainly lack both the ability and the courage to defeat the Tamil minority. They can never defeat the Jaffna Tamils. That is why they are trying to crush and suppress them. What is happening in Sri Lanka today is an open act of genocide. I do not believe the Tamils will wait patiently for very long. In my view, the Tamils deserve greater respect than the Sinhalese. They are entirely worthy of it.”

That is how firmly Lee Kuan Yew expressed himself.

The present Union Government must not forget that, in the Indian Parliament itself on 16 August 1983, the then Prime Minister Indira Gandhi described what was taking place in Sri Lanka as genocide.

During the India–China and India–Pakistan wars, the Sri Lankan Government did not stand with India; it stood with India’s enemies. It was the Eelam Tamils who emotionally and wholeheartedly supported India.

The Union Government must at least now recognise that the Sri Lankan Government and Sinhala nationalism have continually acted against India and the Tamils, and that Sri Lanka displays none of the qualities of a friendly country. Without misplaced leniency, India must firmly question the Sri Lankan Government and exert decisive pressure for the implementation of the Thirteenth Amendment to Sri Lanka’s Constitution. Whatever conciliatory effort India makes, the Sri Lankan Government will portray it as its own daring achievement. Unless India understands this, it will later have cause to regret it.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**

19 August 2013

அப்பட்டமான ஒரு இன அழிப்பு. நிச்சயம் தமிழர்கள்
பொறுமையோடு நீண்ட காலம் காத்திருக்கமாட்டார்கள் என்றே
நான் கருதுகிறேன். என்னைப் பொறுத்தவரை, சிங்களவர்களை
விட தமிழர்களுக்குத்தான் அதிக மரியாதை தரப்பட வேண்டும்.
அதற்கு முற்றிலும் தகுதியானவர்கள் தமிழர்கள்தான்.”
