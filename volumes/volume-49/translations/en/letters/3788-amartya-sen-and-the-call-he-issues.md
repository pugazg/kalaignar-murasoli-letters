# 3788. Amartya Sen — and the Call He Issues!

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil is reproduced in full below the translation and remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.” Literally evoking “one born alongside me,” Kalaignar uses it as a distinctive address of shared identity, equality, affection, and solidarity within the movement.

**Udanpirappē,**

Nakkheeran Kamaraj recently presented me, on the occasion of his birthday, with *An Uncertain Glory—India and Its Contradictions*, written jointly by Nobel laureate Amartya Sen and Jean Drèze.

Amartya Sen is an eminent and renowned professor of economics from West Bengal. He received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1998 and has written economic works acclaimed throughout the world. Jean Drèze, who has previously co-authored several books with him, has lived in India since 1979 and became an Indian citizen in 2002. He is an economist of mature experience who has taught at several major universities.

On page 72 of their book, whose title may be rendered as *Uncertain Glory—India and Its Contradictions*, the authors write that if Indian States were treated as separate countries for the purpose of South Asian comparison, Kerala and Tamil Nadu would stand at the top, while States such as Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh would perform enormously worse. What emerges most powerfully from comparisons among India’s States, they say, is how much a diverse country can learn from the experience of its more successful States.

Although the authors point to many lessons India must learn from international experience, they also stress that there is much to be learnt from the wide range of experience within India itself. The development of Indian States differs greatly from one State to another.

It was in reflection of this very idea that, when I was Chief Minister, I said at a meeting of the National Integration Council in 2008: “Our country contains many peoples, religions, cultures and languages in an integrated whole. This diversity is our strength, not our weakness. We must deepen the desire of all sections of the people to grow and prosper.”

At meetings of both the National Development Council and the National Integration Council, I repeatedly explained that development varies from State to State. Developed States, developing States and underdeveloped States cannot all be placed on the same plane and measured by the same standard. The Union Government must adopt a balanced policy in allocating funds; a State must not be punished merely because it is developed.

For example, at the National Development Council meeting of 9 December 2006, I explained Tamil Nadu’s case by saying that the State had received no additional financial support under the Bharat Nirman programme. A well-performing State such as Tamil Nadu was thereby being indirectly punished for its success. I asked the Prime Minister to grant Tamil Nadu a special exemption under that programme.

Amartya Sen’s book offers numerous statistics to demonstrate the disparities in development among Indian States. In female literacy, Tamil Nadu stands at 69.4 per cent. The corresponding figures cited are only 36.2 per cent in Rajasthan, 49.6 per cent in Andhra Pradesh, 52.8 per cent in Odisha, 59.7 per cent in Karnataka and 58.8 per cent in West Bengal.

Among children aged six to fourteen attending school, Tamil Nadu records 93.9 per cent, Kerala 97.7 per cent and Himachal Pradesh 96.2 per cent. Every other State is shown as having a smaller proportion than these three.

Among children aged eight to eleven who can read well, Tamil Nadu records 79 per cent. Here too, apart from Kerala and Himachal Pradesh, all other States have lower proportions.

The source gives the mortality figure for children under five as 35.5 in Tamil Nadu and 16.3 in Kerala, while citing much higher figures elsewhere: Madhya Pradesh 94.2, Odisha 90.6, Uttar Pradesh 96.4, West Bengal 59.6 and Karnataka 54.7.

The proportion below the poverty line is stated as 29.4 per cent in Tamil Nadu and 19.6 per cent in Kerala, compared with 48.6 per cent in Madhya Pradesh, 40.9 per cent in Uttar Pradesh and 34.2 per cent in West Bengal.

Tamil Nadu’s Human Development Index is given as 0.749. Only Kerala and Himachal Pradesh stand above Tamil Nadu; all the other States are below these three.

Seven large States—Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh—together contain 54.5 crore people, about half of India’s population, and continue to suffer severe poverty. In some of them, the poverty is described as even harsher than conditions prevailing in parts of Africa.

Having cited these figures from Amartya Sen’s book, our Chief Minister may say: “See, see! Karunanidhi himself has shown how far Tamil Nadu has advanced during my two years in office.” But one must remember that the data compiled by Amartya Sen come from years before the AIADMK assumed office in 2011.

After two centuries of colonial rule, India became independent in 1947 and immediately adopted a democratic political system that allowed room for many parties, freedom of speech and broad political rights. The famines of British rule disappeared, and the economy gradually advanced after independence. Economic growth accelerated during the past thirty years, and India became the second fastest-growing large economy among developing countries. Yet the book identifies inadequate attention to the basic needs of the poor and a failure of responsibility towards women’s advancement as among India’s greatest present problems.

It says that insufficient progress has been made in using natural resources to improve living conditions and in increasing popular participation. Problems of safe drinking water, reliable electricity, sanitation, transport and health continue. Amartya Sen also criticises India’s neglect of the development, growth and use of human capabilities of the kind pursued in Japan, South Korea and China.

To rescue India from these fundamental challenges, planning must be reconsidered. Public awareness must be created about regression in social and economic conditions. Debate on major issues is increasingly confined to the privileged; public discussion must also be created and encouraged among those without such advantages. By fully practising political democracy, India can bring about major changes in its foundational conditions.

The book’s argument, as summarised in the letter, is that India has begun a successful chapter in economic growth and poverty reduction, yet remains a major home of global poverty. Half of India’s children are undernourished. Its vast population and excessive poverty remain great challenges.

Commentators have described the book as a subtle study of the Indian economy that also recognises its immense potential; as a deep and accurate criticism of the economy’s present condition; and as a work that treats not only economics but also the finer points of politics, history and law.

Another assessment says that the book has appeared at an appropriate time, when debate over a falling growth rate is intensifying. More troubling than a fall in the growth rate itself, however, is the persistence of social disparities even when growth rises. Indian democracy has failed to meet the socio-economic challenges that have emerged, and inequality is increasing. India’s performance in education, health, women’s literacy and nutrition is described as only slightly better than that of Africa, while Bangladesh has obtained stronger social indicators. India must not assume that it can develop simply by following the American path. Mistaken approaches towards the public and private sectors have been followed from the beginning, and change will not be instantaneous; it can arise only gradually. Raghavan Srinivasan gathered and presented such arguments from Amartya Sen in an article in *The Hindu*.

The English newspaper *The Economic Times* carried a substantial editorial on the book today, 3 August 2013. It said the book emphasised that sustaining development required not only better infrastructure but recognition of the importance of education and investment. No country, it observed, could escape poverty and reach prosperity without fully meeting the basic needs of its people.

Though the book assembles arguments at the level of India as a whole, two ideas compel us in particular to think deeply. The first is its observation that Kerala and Tamil Nadu, if treated as separate countries, could have stood among highly advanced countries. The second is that the States should not all be weighed on one identical scale; separate measures must be devised according to each State’s development and the factors that enable it.

In particular, if all sides come forward to implement projects such as the Sethu project—not trying to obstruct them, but considering their total benefits—I have not the slightest doubt that, as Amartya Sen writes, we can skilfully push aside the challenges before us and continue moving swiftly along the path of progress.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**4 August 2013**

வருகிறது. ஆப்பிரிக்க நாட்டில் காணப்படுவதை விட சற்றே
அதிகமாகத்தான் கல்வி, சுகாதாரம், பெண்கள் எழுத்தறிவு,
சத்துணவு போன்ற துறைகளில் இந்தியா வளர்ந்திருக்கிறது.
வங்காள தேசம்கூட இந்தியாவைவிட சிறந்த சமூகக் குறியீடுகளைப்
பெற்றிருக்கிறது. இந்தியா அமெரிக்கப் பாதையைப் பின்பற்றி
வளர்ச்சி அடைந்து விடலாம் என்று எண்ணி விடக் கூடாது.
பொதுத் துறை மற்றும் தனியார் துறை பொருளாதாரம் பற்றிய
தவறான அணுகுமுறைகளே தொடக்கத்திலிருந்து பின்பற்றப்
பட்டு வருகின்றன. மாற்றம் என்பது உடனடியாக ஏற்பட்டு விடாது;
கொஞ்சம் கொஞ்சமாகத்தான் அது உருவாக முடியும்” என்பது
போன்ற கருத்துக்களை இந்த நூலாசிரியர் அமர்த்தியா சென்

விவரித்திருப்பதையெல்லாம் தொகுத்து, “இந்து” நாளிதழில் திரு.
ரகுவீர் சீனிவாசன் அவர்கள் கட்டுரையாகத் தீட்டியிருக்கிறார்.

இன்றைய (3-8-2013) “எகனாமிக் டைம்ஸ்” ஆங்கில
நாளேட்டில் இந்த நூலைப் பற்றி சிறப்பானதொரு தலையங்கமே
எழுதியிருக்கிறார்கள். அதில், “வளர்ச்சியை ஏற்படுத்தி அதனை
நிலைநிறுத்திக் கொள்ளவேண்டுமேயானால், சிறந்த கட்டமைப்பு
வசதிகளை உருவாக்குவதோடு, கல்வி மற்றும் முதலீடு
ஆகியவற்றின் முக்கியத்துவம் உணரப்படுதல் வேண்டும் என்ற
கருத்தை இந்த நூலில் வலியுறுத்தியிருக்கிறார். மக்களின்
அடிப்படைத் தேவைகளை முழுமையாக நிறைவு செய்யாமல், எந்த
ஒரு நாடும் ஏழ்மை நிலையிலிருந்து மீண்டு, செழிப்பான நிலைக்கு
உயர்ந்துவிட முடியாது என்பதையும் விளக்கியிருக்கிறார்” என்று
குறிப்பிடப்பட்டுள்ளது.

இந்திய அளவில் இப்படிப்பட்ட கருத்துகளையெல்லாம்
தொகுத்திருந்த போதிலும் நம்மைப் பொறுத்தவரையில்
நான் முதலில் எழுதியவாறு; கேரளாவும், தமிழ்நாடும் தனி
நாடாக இருந்திருந்தால், எவ்வளவோ முன்னேற்றம் அடைந்த
நாடாகத் திகழ்ந்திருக்க முடியும் என்ற கருத்தும்; மாநிலங்கள்
அனைத்தையும் ஒரே அளவில் சீர்தூக்கிடாமல், அந்தந்த
மாநிலங்களில் ஏற்பட்டுள்ள வளர்ச்சியையும், வளர்ச்சிக்கான
கூறுகளையும் அடிப்படையாகக் கொண்டு தனித்தனி
அளவுகோல்கள் நிர்ணயிக்கப்பட வேண்டும் என்ற கருத்தும்தான்
நம்மை ஆழ்ந்து சிந்திக்க வைக்கின்றன.

குறிப்பாக சேது திட்டம் போன்றவற்றை தடுக்க நினைக்காமல்;
ஒட்டுமொத்தமாக அவற்றால் ஏற்படக்கூடிய நன்மைகளை மட்டும்
எண்ணிப் பார்த்து நிறைவேற்றிட அனைத்துத் தரப்பினரும் முன்
வருவார்களேயானால் அமர்த்தியா சென் எழுதியிருப்பதைப் போல,
நமக்கு எதிரே உள்ள சவால்களை சாமர்த்தியமாகப் புறந்தள்ளி
முன்னேற்றப் பாதையில் தொடர்ந்து வேகமாக அடியெடுத்து
வைத்திட முடியும் என்பதில் எனக்கு எள்ளளவும் சந்தேகம்
இல்லை.
