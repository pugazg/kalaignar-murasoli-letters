# 3761. Another Attempt to Sell Shares of the Lignite Company?

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil is reproduced in full below the translation and remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.” Literally evoking “one born alongside me,” Kalaignar uses it as a distinctive address of shared identity, equality, affection, and solidarity within the movement.

**Source pages:** [PDF 381](../../../pages/page-381.md)–[PDF 386](../../../pages/page-386.md)  
**Date:** 26 May 2013

**Udanpirappē,**

In a letter to the Prime Minister of India dated 23 May 2013, Tamil Nadu Chief Minister Jayalalithaa wrote that she had learnt that the Union Government was planning to sell a further five per cent of its shares in Neyveli Lignite Corporation, that such a move was undesirable, and that instead of selling NLC shares the government should explore alternative methods. That letter has appeared in all the newspapers.

When the DMK was in power too, on 22 June 2006, the Union Government announced a decision to sell ten per cent of the Neyveli shares. The very next morning, 23 June, the DMK trade union—Labour Progressive Federation—called representatives of CITU, AITUC, INTUC and the Officers' Association for consultations. At that meeting it was decided to begin an indefinite strike from 4 July 2006.

On 25 June 2006 I wrote to the Prime Minister about the issue. I said:

> “The Union Government should not disregard the opposition that has arisen in Tamil Nadu to the proposed sale of ten per cent of the shares in Neyveli. Whatever reason may be given for departing from the Common Minimum Programme already agreed upon, it will be regarded as weakening the commitment to adhere to the policy that was originally accepted. I therefore request the Union Government to reconsider this decision.”

The Common Minimum Programme adopted by the United Progressive Alliance in 2004 had agreed that **profit-making public-sector enterprises would not be privatised and Navratna public-sector companies would remain under government control**.

When there was no immediate reply to my letter, the strike at Neyveli began on the 4th. When journalists asked me about it on 6 July 2006, I said:

> “If the Union Government is in a position where it cannot meet the workers' demand in the Neyveli issue, we are considering whether the DMK should remain part of that government and bear responsibility for the decision taken by it. I have asked the DMK ministers in Delhi to convey this to the Prime Minister.”

Accordingly, Thambi Dayanidhi Maran, who was then a Union Minister, immediately met the Prime Minister and conveyed my view. That very night the Union Government announced that its decision to sell ten per cent of Neyveli Lignite Corporation shares was being put on hold. The workers then withdrew their strike.

Later, 13,000 contract workers of Neyveli Lignite Corporation began a strike from 21 April 2012 pressing a ten-point charter of demands. Writing about this in my *Murasoli* Udanpirappu letter of 8 May 2012, I said that even if the AIADMK State Government was showing no interest in the issue, the Union Government and the minister responsible for the department should immediately contact the Neyveli management, call the protesting workers for talks and move towards an amicable settlement, and that the Prime Minister should take the necessary steps.

During the Assembly session, after Question Hour on 3 May 2012, members of the Communist parties said:

> “Neyveli workers are engaged in a struggle pressing various demands including permanent employment. The Union Government does not appear to be taking any action to bring the workers' struggle to an end. Therefore the State Government must intervene and act.”

Tamil Nadu Labour Welfare Minister C.T. Chellapandian immediately rose and replied:

> “The NLC workers' agitation does not fall within the jurisdiction of the Tamil Nadu Government; therefore we cannot intervene.”

(*Dinamani*, 4 May 2012.)

Yet the same minister who told the Assembly that the State could not intervene said again in the Assembly on 8 May 2012:

> “The Union Government's Labour Welfare Commission must hold talks and find a solution to end the NLC workers' agitation. The DMK, which is part of the Union Government, has not pressed for a solution to this issue. The Tamil Nadu Government will urge the Union Government to find a solution to the NLC workers' agitation.”

(*Dinamani*, 9 May 2012.)

The contract-workers issue in Neyveli Lignite Corporation is one that recurs roughly once every two years. A contract problem that had continued from the time NLC was established was brought under some control in 1994 through the efforts of the Labour Progressive Federation. On that basis the **Neyveli Lignite Corporation Industrial Co-operative Service Society** was established. Five thousand contract workers were registered in it; middlemen **(Contractors)** were removed, and the co-operative society itself paid the workers' wages. Later, whenever vacancies arose in the management, contract workers were progressively regularised.

After 1994, however, the management failed to enrol workers who subsequently came in as contract labour as members of the co-operative society, and the influence of intermediaries began again. This led to an agitation in 2008, during DMK rule. Thirteen thousand workers participated in that strike too.

The DMK, although it was the State Government, did not say that it had no concern in the matter. As Chief Minister, I personally intervened. As a result, on 16 June 2008 the trade unions and management held discussions before the Chief Labour Commissioner in Delhi and entered into a tripartite agreement: 5,000 contract workers would be registered in the co-operative society, and their monthly wages would be increased by ₹750 above what they had been receiving. With that agreement, the strike was withdrawn and the workers returned to duty.

Later, because the management delayed registering contract workers in the co-operative society, workers again went on strike in 2010. I was Chief Minister then too, and on 15 October 2010 I wrote to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh. After that, the management was called for talks and persuaded to accept a monthly wage increase of ₹1,040. Only the leaders of the Labour Progressive Federation accepted that; the other workers continued the strike.

I immediately invited representatives of all trade unions to the Secretariat on 26 October 2010 and held negotiations. I persuaded the management too, raised the increase from ₹1,040 to ₹1,560, and secured several additional concessions. As a result of the negotiations I conducted, the trade-union representatives were satisfied and returned to work. They thanked me. This happened in 2010 under DMK rule.

But in 2012 the management delayed registering contract workers in the Co-operative Service Society, citing pending court cases. The workers therefore began another agitation. Negotiations held in Neyveli failed. Tripartite talks were then held at the office of the Chief Labour Commissioner of the Union Ministry of Labour and Employment at Nungambakkam in Chennai, but no agreement was reached. It was then stated that NLC management should take steps to secure an early disposal of the case pending in the Supreme Court concerning permanent employment for the workers.

I have described above what procedure was followed when Neyveli contract workers went on strike under DMK rule. During AIADMK rule, however, a doubt arose whether there was even such a thing as a Tamil Nadu Government. Yet AIADMK people complained that “it does not even seem as though there is a government at the Centre.” Talks of some kind went on for days at official level, but neither the Chief Minister nor a minister intervened. The agitation finally ended on 5 June 2012.

Meanwhile, on 24 May 2013, Union Minister and friend Narayanasamy expressed a view suggesting that there was nothing wrong in selling NLC shares. This view, hostile to Tamil Nadu including Puducherry and particularly to the workers, is not acceptable to the DMK.

It was because of the strenuous efforts of Thambi Murasoli Maran when he was Union Minister for Industry and Commerce that NLC received **Navratna** status. The Union Government should not again attempt to sell NLC shares. Such a step would unnecessarily drag Tamils—particularly Neyveli workers—into needless trouble. I wish to warn the Union Government not to engage in such an action and thereby invite yet another protest.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**26 May 2013**
