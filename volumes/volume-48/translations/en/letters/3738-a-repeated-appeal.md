# 3738. An Appeal I Make Again and Again!

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil is reproduced in full below the translation and remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.” Literally evoking “one born alongside me,” Kalaignar uses it as a distinctive address of shared identity, equality, affection, and solidarity within the movement.

**Date:** 19 April 2013

**Udanpirappē,**

In 1993, a car-bomb attack was carried out in Delhi in an attempt to kill Congress leader M.S. Bitta. Bitta survived, but nine people were killed. Devender Pal Singh Bhullar, who carried out the attack, fled to Germany. After Bhullar was deported from Germany in 1995, the trial court sentenced him to death in 2001. The Delhi Sessions Court confirmed that sentence in 2002. In the meantime, Bhullar became mentally ill. Seeking release on grounds of mercy, he sent a mercy petition to the President in 2003; in 2011, then President Pratibha Patil rejected it.

Because of the delay in deciding the mercy petition, Bhullar filed a petition in the Supreme Court in September 2011 seeking commutation of his death sentence. That petition was rejected on 12 April 2013. This caused shock throughout Punjab. Some human-rights organisations have demanded that the mentally ill Bhullar not be executed. The ruling party in Punjab, the Shiromani Akali Dal, has strongly pressed this view. Punjab Chief Minister Parkash Singh Badal and his son, Deputy Chief Minister Sukhbir Singh, personally met Prime Minister Manmohan Singh and Union Home Minister Sushilkumar Shinde and urged them to show mercy to Bhullar. They said the issue had created an agitated situation in Punjab and requested mercy for Bhullar in order to help preserve peace in the State.

When reporters asked Union Home Minister Shinde about this, he said, “The Punjab Chief Minister's request will be considered.” That announcement by the Union Minister has given human-rights activists some consolation and hope.

From the Chief Minister down, the people of Punjab are speaking with one voice to ask that the death sentence of a person from their State be commuted to life imprisonment. But what is the situation in Tamil Nadu?

In a statement I issued on 29 August 2011, I said:

> “There is hardly anyone who has not expressed the view that the death penalty, which is opposed to human rights and humanitarianism, is unnecessary and that every country could abolish it. Instead of the maximum punishment of death, if a prisoner accused of murder is made to spend the rest of his life thinking again and again about that crime and grieving within himself, the death penalty is not going to produce some greater benefit; therefore it should be commuted to life imprisonment. If a man has indeed committed murder, spending an entire lifetime regretting and suffering for it is not merely harsher than a death sentence—it can also give him an opportunity and a path to reform himself and live differently.
>
> “The moving letters we wrote to the Union Government, and the appeals we personally placed before the Prime Minister and all the leaders of India, are known to everyone. I appeal that the lives of the three men said to be about to be taken to the gallows—Santhan, Perarivalan and Murugan—be saved. I think that even if the young leader Rajiv Gandhi, who is no longer among us, were alive today, in this situation he would have respected the voice of true Tamils and, in keeping with Anna's golden words, ‘Let us forget; let us forgive,’ that great man would have saved the lives of these three.
>
> “If their death sentences are commuted—and if they did in fact commit the offence—then the more than twenty years they have already spent in prison in a state of remorse should be regarded as punishment greater than life imprisonment. If the death sentences are cancelled, the Tamil people will be a people who wholeheartedly praise and rejoice in this humane act. I therefore once again strongly appeal to the Union and State governments to save these three from the grip of death. I make this appeal also as one who knows what pure lives people released from the gallows have gone on to lead, and as one who was among those responsible for such releases.”

When the Assembly passed a resolution concerning the release of these three, Jayalalithaa said in her speech that under Article 161 of the Constitution the State Chief Minister had no power in the matter, and that the State Governor too had no power. To this day, Jayalalithaa has continued to repeat the same view—that the State Chief Minister has no authority in this matter.

Yet in the statement she read in the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly under Rule 110 on 30 August 2011, she herself said that His Excellency the Governor had accepted the decision of the Cabinet headed by Karunanidhi and given approval on 21 April 2000. It is in precisely that manner, out of concern to save the lives of these three, that I and several other parties in Tamil Nadu have continued to demand that the Tamil Nadu government and Jayalalithaa pass a resolution in the State Cabinet and send it to the Governor for recommendation.

Likewise, we have continuously appealed for mercy for four men sentenced to death after being accused of being associates of Veerappan, and have asked that their death sentences be commuted to life imprisonment. The Mysore TADA Court that tried Gnanaprakasam, Simon, Meesai Mathaiyan and Bilavendran sentenced them only to life imprisonment. But because that judgment was appealed in the Supreme Court, the Supreme Court in 2004 converted the life sentences imposed on the four into death sentences.

Nine years have passed since that judgment, and the death sentences have not been carried out. In the meantime, all four sent mercy petitions to the President of India. After those petitions remained pending for a long time, the President rejected them on 11 February 2013.

On behalf of the four men, a petition was filed in the Supreme Court on 16 February 2013 opposing the death sentences and seeking a stay on their execution. The petition also asked that the death sentences be commuted to life imprisonment. Their lawyers, Colin Gonsalves and Samik Narayan, went to the residence of Chief Justice Altamas Kabir to file the petition and sought an immediate hearing. The Supreme Court has deferred its decision concerning these four men.

In 2007, when the United Nations put forward a resolution against the death penalty, 104 countries supported and signed it; only 39 countries, including India, voted against it. Following the U.N. resolution, the source says, 90 per cent of the world's countries have abolished the death penalty. Should India not join that majority?

Those in positions of responsibility should bear in mind the principle that even if the guilty escape, the innocent must not be punished. When Bhullar's punishment is taken up for reconsideration, the death sentences of these four men and those of Perarivalan, Santhan and Murugan should also be considered together and commuted. Once again I strongly urge that the death penalty itself should, in general, be abolished altogether, and I appeal to the Union and State governments to show the way toward that end.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**19 April 2013**
