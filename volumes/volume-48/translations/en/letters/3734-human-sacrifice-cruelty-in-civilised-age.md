# 3734. The Cruelty of Human Sacrifice in a Civilised Age?

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil is reproduced in full below the translation and remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.” Literally evoking “one born alongside me,” Kalaignar uses it as a distinctive address of shared identity, equality, affection, and solidarity within the movement.

**Date:** 15 April 2013

**Udanpirappē,**

Today (14 April 2013), I saw in a Tamil newspaper a report about a child being killed as a human sacrifice.

Senthil, a construction worker, lives in Vyasarpadi, Chennai. His wife's name is Geetha. They had a two-and-a-half-year-old son named Vishnu. On 9 April 2013, the child Vishnu was found submerged in a water barrel on the terrace of the neighbouring house. Relatives who saw him took the child to hospital. But he died on the way.

Believing that Vishnu had accidentally fallen into the water and died, his parents cremated the body. Meanwhile, a fortune-teller in that area told neighbours that the child had in fact been pushed into a water tank and killed by his younger grandmother, Maheswari. The police too were informed.

When the police brought Maheswari in for questioning, she reportedly admitted killing the child and gave a statement. In that statement she said: “Although there had been differences between Senthil's family and me, Senthil's son Vishnu would always play with me. On the night of 7 April 2013, I held out my hand to a kudukuduppai fortune-teller who came to our street and had my fortune read. He told me that someone had placed ‘seivinai’ upon me and my family, and said that if I paid ₹2,000 he would remove it.

“The next day the kudukuduppai man gave me a lemon over which incantations had been performed, two amulets, kumkum and similar objects, and told me to tie the amulets on myself and my child. He took ₹1,000 from me. A short while after tying the amulet, I lost awareness of myself. Whenever I saw the child Vishnu, I felt angry. I felt that I could kill the child. On 9 April 2013, I brought the child with me and tried to kill him by forcing him into the water tank in the house. The child struggled for life, unable to breathe. I immediately carried him to the terrace of the neighbouring house, put him head-first into the water barrel there, and staged it to look as though he had accidentally fallen in. Everyone believed it. They took the child to hospital. But Vishnu died.”

A murder case has been registered against Maheswari and she has been arrested. People who saw Vishnu when he was taken to hospital say there were injuries on his face, hands and legs. People in the locality believe that Maheswari may have killed the child as a human sacrifice in an attempt to remove a supposed “dosham”.

When I read this report, the doubt pierced my thoughts: are we really living in the twenty-first century? This is an age in which science has advanced enormously. We have reached a stage where it has been established that almost nothing lies beyond human knowledge. The evolution of living beings, comprehensive study of the planets, the science called information technology reducing this entire world to a small village and bringing the world into the palm of one's hand—human knowledge continues to expand in depth and breadth beyond any boundary one might try to draw.

Yet even as we see and hear all this, marvelling at and celebrating the greatness of human knowledge, when reports of “seivinai—kudukuduppai fortune-teller—amulet—dosham—human sacrifice” fall upon our ears, one cannot help wondering whether, even after science has risen to astonishing heights, a section of humankind has still not escaped from the abyss called superstition.

From time immemorial, Thanthai Periyar and, following him, the Self-Respect Movement have carried on immense rationalist propaganda. Yet even after all that, superstition—like a disease that kills one born alongside us, like weeds springing up in a good field—continues here and there to eat away at society. Completely uprooting this weed of superstition is among the finest forms of social service. It is an essential task that everyone must undertake, beyond politics and beyond differences of opinion.

Thanthai Periyar said: “Only on the day all our people free themselves from superstitious customs and practices can we say that our country has attained true liberation.” Let us all remember those words and work tirelessly; that is what will serve the advancement of our society.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**15 April 2013**

## Letter-specific notes

1. **Udanpirappē** is retained as Kalaignar's characteristic political-familial address.
2. **seivinai** is retained because the source invokes the folk-superstitious idea of harmful occult action directed at a person or family; the translation does not validate the belief.
3. **kudukuduppai** identifies the itinerant fortune-teller described in the source, traditionally associated with a small percussion instrument.
4. **dosham** is retained as the source's term for an alleged harmful affliction or defect in the superstitious account.
