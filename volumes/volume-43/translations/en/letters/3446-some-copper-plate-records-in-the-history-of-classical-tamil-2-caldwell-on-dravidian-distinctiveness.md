# 3446. Some Copper-Plate Records in the History of Classical Tamil — 2: Caldwell on Dravidian Distinctiveness

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This second letter in the `செப்பேடுகள்` series moves from Parithimar Kalaignar to Robert Caldwell and then to institutional resolutions from 1918 onward. Source-supplied English from Caldwell is retained as printed rather than silently rewritten; older Tamil in quoted resolutions is translated for meaning while the exact audited Tamil remains below.

**Tamil source:** [Letter 3446](../../../chapters/3446-semmoli-varalatril-sila-seppedugal-2-caldwell-kaattiya-dravida-thanichirappu.md)  
**Source pages:** [PDF 157](../../../pages/page-157.md)–[PDF 164](../../../pages/page-164.md)  
**Date:** 26 December 2009

**Udanpirappē,**

If Parithimar Kalaignar was the Tamil who first raised the call that Tamil is a classical language, the first foreign scholar to say that Tamil is a classical language was the scholar Robert Caldwell. Thirty years before Parithimar Kalaignar raised his voice in 1887, Caldwell, in 1856, wrote in his incomparable and monumental work *A Comparative Grammar of the Dravidian Languages*:

> “Among all the Dravidian languages, Tamil, which stands established as a high, independent classical language, can live after completely casting out the Sanskrit words that have entered it and can prosper and grow without needing their help in the slightest.
>
> “The ancient or classical dialect of the Tamil language, called Shen-Tamil or correct Tamil, in which nearly all the literature has been written, contains exceedingly little Sanskrit; and differs from the colloquial dialect, or the language of prose, chiefly in the sedulous and jealous care with which it has rejected the use of Sanskrit derivatives and characters, and restricted itself to pure Ancient Dravidian sounds, forms, and roots)”

Thus he demonstrated with supporting evidence that Tamil is indeed a classical language.

The antiquity, purity, strength and beauty of Tamil are such as to draw all who learn it and make them feel it dearer than life itself. Many foreigners were thus drawn to Tamil; foremost among them was Caldwell. Dr Caldwell, from Ireland, came to Chennai in 1838 at the age of 24 as a missionary through the London Missionary Society. Later, while serving as a bishop in Tirunelveli, he studied hundreds of palm-leaf manuscripts and Sangam-age Tamil literature, undertook archaeological research, discovered coins bearing the fish emblem of the Pandya period, researched the history of Tirunelveli, and wrote a book on that history based on his studies. It was Caldwell who coined the expression “Dravidian Languages.”

The works produced through Caldwell’s deep research also came to encourage the non-Brahmin movement in Tamil Nadu. Because of his attachment to Tamil, Tamil civilisation and culture, Arignar Anna had Caldwell’s statue installed on Chennai’s Marina beach during the Second World Tamil Conference. The statue of Robert Caldwell donated by the Church of South India was unveiled on 2.1.1968 by multilingual scholar K. Appadurai under the chairmanship of the then Chairman of the Tamil Nadu Legislative Council, M.A. Manickavelar.

For a scholar of such profound learning and renown to have declared Tamil a classical language is, in the history of the classical-language movement, a great golden record that has come down to us like a treasure of gems.

Another resolution, adopted as early as 1918 by the Saiva Siddhanta Maha Samaj, decisively affirming Tamil as a classical language, deserves to be remembered with great joy and fulfilment by Tamil enthusiasts and well-wishers. The account of that resolution reads as follows:

> “Under the auspices of the Madras Saiva Siddhanta Maha Samaj, at 5.45 p.m. on Friday (15.3.1918), nearly 400 Tamil people gathered in the Pachaiyappa’s College hall for the purpose of adopting certain resolutions relating to the Tamil language. Many pandits, vidwans and English-educated graduates distinguished the meeting. Chandler, M.A., President of the Tamil Lexicon Committee, took the chair and, after speaking briefly on the character of Tamil, Resolution 1 was moved.
>
> “Tamil alone, among the Dravidian languages, shines as a high independent classical language possessing antiquity, refinement and leadership. Possessing letters formed from natural sounds; enduring in continuous usage; having from ancient times a script of its own; possessing an abundance of ancient works of grammar, literature and subtle learning; and shining with its own distinctive subtlety of inner themes, brilliance of outer themes and excellence of moral works, it should be honoured by the university alongside other independent languages such as Sanskrit, Arabic and Persian.”

K. Subramania Pillai, M.A., M.L., moved this first resolution. In support, he said that Tamil was a language of very great antiquity and distinction; that references to Dravidian works in certain Sanskrit Upanishads themselves revealed its antiquity; that the ancient Tamil grammar *Tolkappiyam* had been composed before the Sanskrit grammar of Panini; that Tamil letters were formed from natural sounds; and that deeper study of Tamil works would reveal their subtleties.

R. P. Sethu Pillai, B.A., seconding the proposal, said that Tamil possessed an elevation and refinement worthy of being counted among the other high independent classical languages. Though many scholars of the day accepted Tamil as an independent southern language, those who argued that it was not a high independent classical language merely because it existed both in literary and worldly usage did not understand the true definition of a classical language. A language possessing refined character and distinguished civilisation was entitled to that name; Tamil’s refined character was evident from the precision and subtlety of its ancient grammar *Tolkappiyam*. Neutral English authors themselves had said that Tamil should not be treated like other Indian languages lacking such grammatical limits. The excellence of Tamil works, the subtleties of inner and outer themes, and their verbal and semantic beauty revealed its refined civilisation. The fact that the *Tirukkural*, one of the ancient Tamil works, had been translated into many European languages then in use itself demonstrated the language’s stature. And just as other classical languages had daughter languages, Tamil had Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Tulu and other Dravidian languages.

He further argued that Tamil’s antiquity was amply shown by *Tolkappiyam*, composed about 2,500 years ago: grammar is formulated only after literature exists, and grammar is drawn from literature, so Tamil must have possessed a flourishing body of literature far beyond the limits of recorded history. Therefore, because Tamil possessed antiquity, refinement and cultivated character, the university ought necessarily to count it among the high independent classical languages.

After this, E. Suvara Iyer, editor of *Tamil Kalanjiyam*, seconded the resolution, saying that there was sufficient evidence that Tamil was an independent language. Pandita Mrs Alarmelmangai Thayar then said that some members of the university who had failed to understand the excellence of Tamil spoke disparagingly of it; since Tamil possessed the stature to be counted among the great languages, the university must recognise it. The first resolution was then unanimously approved by all the Tamil people assembled there.

In his diary entry concerning this Pachaiyappa’s College meeting, the Pure Tamil scholar Maraimalai Adigal noted on 12.3.1918 that K. Subramania Pillai, M.A., M.L., had asked him to speak at the meeting to be held on the 15th (15.3.1918) to petition the government to accept Tamil as a “Classical Language.”

The contribution of the Thanjavur–Karanthai Tamil Sangam to the history of Tamil’s classical-language recognition is not merely worthy of praise; it is something Tamils cannot forget as long as they live on earth. The Karanthai Tamil Sangam was founded in 1911 by T. V. Radhakrishna Pillai and other people with enthusiasm and training in Tamil. Tamil scholar T. V. Umamaheswaram Pillai served as its president from its beginning until the end of his life and rendered distinguished service.

That is why, when I spoke at the function held on 18.2.2006 in Kalaignar Arangam at Anna Arivalayam in Chennai for the release of a postage stamp honouring Tamilavel Umamaheswaranar, I said:

> “This is a period in which the name of Umamaheswaranar must come to mind more than the names of other scholars. The reason is that, when we think of Tamil as a classical language, after Parithimar Kalaignar the name that comes to mind is that of Tamilavel Umamaheswaranar. It was he who moved the resolution in the Karanthai Tamil Sangam that Tamil should be made a classical language. Otherwise, on what evidence could we have spoken to the government at the Centre about Tamil becoming a classical language?”

The adornment I thus gave to the greatness of Karanthai Umamaheswaranar remains deeply imprinted in my memory.

The seventh and eighth annual celebrations of the Thanjavur–Karanthai Tamil Sangam were held on 24.5.1919 and 25.5.1919 under the chairmanship of Sivashanmuga Meygnana Sivacharya Swamigal, head of the Tirukkoilur Adheenam’s Tiruppadiripuliyur Jnaniyar Mutt, in the presence of Tamilavel T. V. Umamaheswaram Pillai, Tamil scholar Venkatasamy Nattar, T. N. Gurumurthi Pillai, T. Kurathazhvar Mudaliar and others.

At that celebration a resolution insisted that, since Tamil had been firmly accepted by people of many kinds as a high independent classical language possessing antiquity, refinement and excellence, the University of Madras should change the policy it had followed until then, acknowledge Tamil as such, and give it due first place and rights in the university of this southern land. T. A. Sabapathi Pillai moved the resolution and M. Appavu Mudaliar seconded it. After its adoption, it was formally sent to the Chancellor, Vice-Chancellor and Registrar of the University of Madras and to the Governor-in-Council of Madras.

This was a great resolution that stands as an important milestone in the history of Tamil’s classical-language recognition, because it was the first resolution adopted through an organisation demanding that Tamil be declared a high independent classical language.

The ninth annual celebration of the Thanjavur–Karanthai Tamil Sangam was then held on 21.8.1920 under the chairmanship of S. Somasundara Bharatiar. At this celebration too, a resolution stated that it was wrong that, despite repeated insistence that the exceedingly sweet Tamil language be included in the rank of high independent classical languages, the University of Madras had still failed to do so or make it a compulsory subject. The gathering called upon the university at least thereafter to recognise Tamil as a high independent classical language, make it compulsory and, as far as possible, use it in colleges as the medium for learning the arts. V. A. Sagaraya Pillai moved the resolution and I. Kumarasamy Pillai seconded it.

In 1921, when the head of the Madras Presidency government visited Thanjavur, a delegation led by Rao Bahadur V. A. Vandayar met him on behalf of the Karanthai Tamil Sangam and directly presented its demand that refined Tamil be declared a high independent classical language.

The eleventh annual celebration of the Karanthai Tamil Sangam was held on 18.11.1922 and 19.11.1922 under the chairmanship of K. Subramania Pillai. A resolution adopted there urged Visva-Bharati to accept Tamil as a high independent classical language and include it in its curriculum; it further said that the history of India would be incomplete without South Indian history, that essential information for it was available only in Sangam Tamil works, and that Tamil books and knowledge of Tamil should be spread more widely in the civilised countries of the world.

At the Sangam’s twelfth annual celebration, held on 22.9.1923 and 23.9.1923 under the chairmanship of Tamil patron Sa. Rama. Mu. Chid. Pethachi Chettiar, another resolution said that, because Tamil possessed every qualification to be regarded as the foremost among the high independent classical languages of the world, the Government of India should be urged to recognise it as such and accept it as a subject for the I.C.S. examination.

At the silver jubilee of the Karanthai Tamil Sangam held on 15, 16 and 17 April 1938, Tiruppadiripuliyur Jnaniyar Adigal, who presided, expressed his deep regret that a Tamil language of such greatness and sweetness had not been honoured by the government in the status of a high independent classical language.

Thus, under the leadership of Tamilavel Umamaheswaranar, the Karanthai Tamil Sangam rendered immeasurable service in the early stages of the effort to secure political recognition and proper honour for Tamil as a classical language. In the history of that movement, the Karanthai Tamil Sangam occupies a powerful chapter.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**
