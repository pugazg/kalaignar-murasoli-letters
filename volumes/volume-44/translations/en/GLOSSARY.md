## Source-English policy

Printed English that functions as source wording is preserved rather than silently rewritten. Examples now include:

- `You (Jayalalithaa) are making a mockery of the Judicial Process. How long you can drag the proceedings?`
- `(Creamy Layer)`
- `(Street House)`
- `(SLPs)`
- the *Frontline* English passage beginning `If the AIADMK Government in Tamil Nadu finds itself isolated today...`
- `It has been laid down that if a State wants to exceed 50 percent reservation, then it is required to base its decision on the quantifiable data`
- `(Caste-Based Census)`
- the 3492 source-English passage beginning `I firmly believe that caste-based census will certainly go a long way...`, including its printed hyphenation anomalies.
- `(Supplementary Facility)` in Letter 3499.

## Political and rhetorical language

- Preserve accusation, irony, repetition, rhetorical questions and image-bearing idioms instead of neutralizing them.
- Translate idioms for intelligible English while retaining their source image where practical; for example `வாய் புளித்ததோ, மாங்காய் புளித்ததோ` keeps the “mouth / mango sour” image.
- Do not introduce outside explanations into names, films, publications, schemes or historical claims unless a separate note is explicitly necessary and source-supported.
- Figures, dates, lists and quotations remain in source order and are not silently reconciled against external records.
- Source-specific abusive or historically loaded terms are not silently replaced with a different insult; where a direct English gloss would add meaning not fixed by the source, transliteration plus a note is preferred.
- When the source itself marks a period-specific official, political or community naming choice, preserve that naming choice rather than silently substituting a modern preferred label.
- When an audited Tamil passage is internally opaque, surface the uncertainty and avoid inventing a repair from outside knowledge.
