# 3457. He Laughed; I Laughed Too!

> **Translator’s note**
>
> The source presents this letter as an extended political allegory. It does not name the real-world identities behind the “queen,” her companion or the two ascetics, so the English does not insert them. The temple-tank tragedy, the later murder-case retaliation, the devotional refrain and the final irony about `இன ஒற்றுமை` are preserved in the source’s sequence.

**Tamil source:** [Letter 3457](../../../chapters/3457-avanum-siriththaan-naanum-siriththen.md)  
**Source pages:** [PDF 250](../../../pages/page-250.md)–[PDF 253](../../../pages/page-253.md)  
**Date:** 12 February 2010

**Udanpirappē,**

The kurinji flower has the distinction of blooming only once in twelve years.

The Kumbakonam Mahamaham too is spoken of as having such a distinction.

Somewhere there is a temple! Opposite it, a tank! Even if that tank is filled with mud and slush, and if one adds to that filth the dirt washed off by the thousands of devotees who bathe in it, it would look not like a tank but like a muddy pond. Yet ardent devotees and ordinary devotees still continue to bathe there in the belief that bathing — immersing themselves — in it will wash away sins and accumulate merit.

To such a sin-cleansing sacred tank, a queen went, taking her companion with her, as though saying, “Come, dear, let us bathe and immerse ourselves!” In that divine tank, where a great crowd of people were taking their sacred bath, there suddenly arose a great commotion as though a bomb had exploded. The crush, shouting and confusion created by people running here and there in panic combined so that, at the very place to which people had come to rid themselves of sin, many lives were sacrificed.

In a pavilion beside the tank, two ascetics watched the scene with tears in their eyes. On a raised platform by the temple tower, the queen and her companion happily played, repeatedly scooping up water and pouring it over each other’s heads. The multitudes of devotees who had gathered in the sacred tank to wash away their sins forgot why they had come and felt that merely watching the companion perform an abhishekam for the queen — and the queen in turn perform one for her companion — had cleansed them of every sin.

Even after the commotion and panic caused by the confusion, like a bomb falling and exploding, had subsided, the sight of the queen bathing in the sacred waters with her companion did not fade from the devotees’ minds.

The elder swami and the younger swami, who had kept watching these pitiable scenes, looked at one another and slowly walked away from the bank, climbing into their monastery palanquin. Along the route of the palanquin, newspaper reporters surrounded the swamis and, with their customary skill, asked: “What happened in the sin-cleansing tank? Did you see it directly? How did it happen? Why did it happen?”

The swamis replied: “We know nothing. As far as we saw while standing by the tank, the queen came — her companion came — both kept scooping up water; she poured it over the other’s head, and the other poured it over hers. While the two women were bathing in this way, people fell over one another and pushed forward to see the sight, creating confusion! Bodies piled up!” Having said this, they looked at the palanquin bearers and urged, “Go — go,” and the bearers, frightened, ran. The reporters who had taken notes treated the answer given by the two ascetics as sacred scripture and published it in the newspapers for which they worked.

When the queen read it, she became terribly angry. “Both these swamis have built their monastery on lands under our administration. Did you see how arrogant they are? Let the time come; I will deal with them,” the queen vowed.

The occasion for fulfilling that vow arose. A swami became implicated in a murder committed at the monastery.

“You mocked me, saying, ‘She poured water on her head — and she poured water on the other’s head!’ How arrogant must you have been to play like this with me?” the queen threatened and hounded the swami. “Just watch — I will make you a prisoner under sentence of death,” she taunted.

The swami trembled and wilted. “Alas! We mocked the divine sport the queen conducted in the sacred tank, and now that has returned as an evil fate upon our own head,” he lamented endlessly.

The swami’s true devotees too prayed to God day and night only that somehow their swami should survive and that the accusation placed upon him should disappear. None of them panicked that the queen had thus taken revenge upon the swami they loved, the swami who was their guide; nor did they launch arrows of attack against the queen. They merely raised the song “Jaya Jaya Sankara” loudly, seeking somehow to bow at the queen’s feet, obtain her grace and dispel the delusion of a death sentence threatening the swami. The queen acted as though she had no desire even to hear that song. She ground her teeth. She exploded like a thunderous firecracker.

Between these two contrasting scenes, from the village-council loudspeaker mounted on a pole in the middle of the village, a song began to sound:

> “Are we not both of one clan?
> Shall those who oppose us defeat us?”

And it continued:

> “It was indeed a sin to cast a murder accusation!
> But to make that accusation permanent —
> is that not an even greater sin? Therefore,
> in the pond that washes away wicked sins,
> let us wash away all our sins —
> let us see who the enemies of our community are —
> let us dance and bring them down!”

The song kept resounding through the loudspeaker!

Just then I heard the sound of laughter beside me.

“What, sir? Did you hear the song?” that friend asked, laughing as he patted me on the back.

“My dear fellow, this is what is called ‘community unity,’” I said.

He laughed —

I laughed too!

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**12-02-2010**

## Letter-specific notes

1. The allegorical figures remain unnamed because the source itself leaves them as the queen, her companion and the two ascetics.
2. The temple-tank deaths, the reporters’ account, the queen’s vow and the murder-case sequence remain source-framed; no outside identification or factual reconciliation is added.
3. The closing `இன ஒற்றுமை` is rendered **community unity** in the immediate clan/community rhetoric of the allegory rather than expanded into an external political explanation.
4. No canonical Tamil change was required during this source-check.

அமைக்கப்பட்டிருந்த ஊராட்சி மன்ற ஒலி பெருக்கி குழாயில்

“நாம் இருவரும் ஒரு குலம் அல்லவா?
நம்மை எதிர்ப்போர் நம்மை வெல்லவா?”

என்று ஒரு பாட்டு ஒலிக்க ஆரம்பித்து - தொடர்ந்து;

“கொலைப் பழி தூற்றியது பாவம் தான்!
அதை - நிலைப் பழியாக நிறுத்துவது
அதை விடப் பாவம் அன்றோ? எனவே
பொல்லாப் பாவங்கள் தீர்க்கும்
பொய்கையிலே - நம்
எல்லா பாவங்களையும் தீர்த்திடுவோம் - நம்

இன எதிரிகள் யாரென்று பார்த்திடுவோம் -
கூத்திடுவோம் அவர்களை வீழ்த்திடுவோம் என”

பாடல் முழங்கிக் கொண்டே இருந்தது ஒலிபெருக்கியில்!

அப்போது என்னருகே ஒரு சிரிப்பொலி சப்தம் கேட்டது.

“என்னய்யா? பாடல் கேட்டாயா?” என்று சிரித்துக் கொண்டே
அந்த நண்பன் என் முதுகில் தட்டினான்.

“இதற்குப் பெயர்தானப்பா “இன ஒற்றுமை” என்றேன்.

அவனும் சிரித்தான் -

நானும் சிரித்தேன்!
