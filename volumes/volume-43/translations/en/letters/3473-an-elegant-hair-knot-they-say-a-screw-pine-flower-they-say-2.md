# 3473. An Elegant Hair-Knot, They Say; a Screw-Pine Flower, They Say... (2)

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This is the second letter in the three-part `ஒய்யாரக் கொண்டையாம், தாழம்பூவாம்...` sequence. The actual letter-start title at PDF 332 uses short `ஒய்யாரக்...`, while the printed contents independently uses long `ஓய்யாரக்...`; both layers remain distinct. The letter continues the source’s account of the TANSI appeal, Supreme Court interventions in 2001, Jayalalithaa’s removal from the Chief Minister’s office, the later High Court acquittal, and the Supreme Court’s 24 November 2003 observations. Court and political claims are retained as attributed source material rather than independently reconciled.

**Tamil source:** [Letter 3473](../../../chapters/3473-oyyaarak-kondaiyaam-thaazhampoovaam-2.md)  
**Source pages:** [PDF 332](../../../pages/page-332.md)–[PDF 339](../../../pages/page-339.md)  
**Date:** 31 May 2010

**Udanpirappē,**

When Jayalalithaa was Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu, the Special Court sentenced her to three years’ imprisonment in the case concerning the purchase, at a reduced price, of TANSI land belonging to the Government. Hearings on the appeals filed by Jayalalithaa against that sentence in the Madras High Court began on 27-8-2001.

After the AIADMK won the election, she assumed the office of Chief Minister in great haste. Since she had to become a member of the Legislative Assembly within six months of taking office, she took steps to have the appeal then pending before the High Court heard quickly and to obtain a judgment.

The source says she attempted to make a mockery of the judicial process by having the power to appoint for herself the Government lawyer who was supposed to argue against her in the case. Because a case was filed opposing that, the Madras High Court appointed former Advocate General Venkadapathy as Government counsel. In that situation, the TANSI land-deal appeal began moving rapidly in the Madras High Court.

Venkadapathy, who had been appointed Government counsel, filed a petition in the Supreme Court objecting to the manner in which the TANSI land-deal case was being heard and to Justice Balasubramaniam not giving him time to read the 2,200 pages of documents. In that petition he asked that the case be transferred to a High Court in another State. He said justice could not be sustained if the hearing continued in the Madras High Court; that appeals Jayalalithaa had filed in October 2000 had been taken up in great haste after she became Chief Minister; that after becoming Chief Minister she had decided to appoint even the Government counsel who would argue against her; that he himself had been appointed Government counsel only after a public-interest petition was filed; and therefore the Madras High Court proceedings should be stayed and the case heard in another State.

The petition came before a Division Bench of Supreme Court Judges S.P. Bharucha, Y.K. Sabharwal and Ashok Bhan, and an interim stay was imposed on the Madras High Court hearing of Jayalalithaa’s appeals.

Arguing in that case, Attorney General Soli Sorabjee said, in the source’s Tamil rendering: “What should one call the fact that not even time to read 2,200 pages of documents was given — distressing or farcical? Such an occurrence is highly unusual. Moreover, the Chief Minister of the State herself is the appellant. Therefore, in the interests of justice, it is necessary that these cases be transferred quickly to a High Court in another State. Otherwise the trust and respect the people have in the judiciary will be damaged.”

When I was asked for my view on the Supreme Court’s interim stay, I said I did not wish to say anything about a court order. Even so, in an editorial dated 1-9-2001, *The Hindu* wrote that the Supreme Court was right to stay Jayalalithaa’s appeals because the hearing in the Madras High Court had been proceeding in great haste.

Whether in Parliament or in Legislative Assemblies, it is convention that judges should not be discussed. Yet on 1-9-2001, while Chief Minister, Jayalalithaa made the following statement in the Assembly about a Supreme Court judge:

“The Supreme Court set aside the judgment given by the High Court and ordered a fresh hearing of the case. How did they obtain that order? Did they obtain it by using their influence? They themselves must explain. In the appeal to the Supreme Court against the judgments of the Special Court and the High Court, both judgments were set aside and a fresh hearing was ordered. The DMK people must explain how that order came to be given.”

Thus, in the Legislative Assembly itself, Jayalalithaa imputed a motive even to a Supreme Court order.

But did she stand firmly by what she had said in the Assembly about Supreme Court judges? No. On 3-9-2001, in the same Assembly, she gave a personal explanation under Assembly Rule 109. In it, she said newspapers had reported her remarks about judges in a manner that gave them a different meaning.

On Jayalalithaa’s review petition seeking removal of the interim stay, a Bench comprising Supreme Court Judges Bharucha, Brijesh Kumar and Ashok Bhan ruled on 7-9-2001 that Justice Balasubramaniam, who had been hearing the case in the Madras High Court, should not continue to hear it; that after the new Chief Justice of the Madras High Court took office, the Chief Justice should announce within one week which judge would hear the case; that only thereafter would the hearing begin afresh; and that the fresh hearing should not begin before 1 October.

Writing an editorial about this order, *The Hindu* said that once the situation had arisen in which there was no possibility of Jayalalithaa being elected to the Assembly within six months, she should under no circumstances cling to the office of Chief Minister. It further wrote that while hearing the cases challenging her appointment as Chief Minister, the Supreme Court had raised for consideration the question whether it was proper for a person facing disqualification because of a corruption conviction to take office as Chief Minister. Therefore, instead of continuing in office until the six-month period ended on 14 November and then facing removal, Jayalalithaa should resign immediately, the paper wrote unequivocally.

In the case filed claiming that Jayalalithaa’s appointment as Chief Minister was legally invalid, a unanimous judgment by five Supreme Court judges headed by Justice S.P. Bharucha on 21-9-2001 stated, in the source’s rendering: “Jayalalithaa’s appointment as Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu is invalid. What then-Governor Fathima Beevi did on May 14 was contrary to the Constitution. Jayalalithaa cannot continue any further as Chief Minister, because her appointment on May 14 was not legally valid.”

When Jayalalithaa’s lawyer K.K. Venugopal asked the judges for a few days to make alternative arrangements, they refused the request, saying: “When a Chief Minister dies, an alternative arrangement is made immediately. A party with such a large majority can choose a new leader at any time.”

It is such a person, deserving of this kind of praise (?), who is now inciting her party people to demonstrate and demand that Minister Thambi Raja accept moral responsibility and resign.

At a time when newspapers and lawyers were expressing the view that, on the basis of the Supreme Court ruling, Jayalalithaa should immediately leave the Chief Minister’s office, the AIADMK Legislative Assembly member elected from Andipatti resigned his seat.

At the same time, a case was also filed in the Madras High Court seeking an interim stay of Section 8(3) of the Representation of the People Act, under which persons convicted in cases are barred from contesting elections. When reporters asked me about it, I said: “That law has existed for fifty years. Asking for a licence so that wrongdoers, offenders and those convicted in criminal cases may all contest elections is not good for democracy.”

When reporters asked whether President’s Rule should be imposed in Tamil Nadu because of the Supreme Court judgment, I said: “I do not think so. The AIADMK has a majority in the Assembly. There is nothing wrong with their democratically choosing another person and placing that person in charge.” When asked whether I was happy that justice had been obtained, I said the law had done its duty; I felt neither happiness nor sorrow.

Lawyer Selvaraj of Tamil Nadu, who had filed a case in the Supreme Court seeking Jayalalithaa’s removal from the office of Chief Minister, said the Supreme Court had protected purity in politics and that the judgment was politically significant.

But AIADMK members held processions against the Supreme Court judgment; in town after town, in the presence of police, they burned effigies of me, Murasoli Maran and then Prime Minister Vajpayee. At the AIADMK demonstration in front of Shastri Bhavan, Finance Minister Ponnaiyan himself led the protest and raised slogans with the support of other ministers.

In Jayalalithaa’s appeal against the three-year sentence imposed by the Special Court in the TANSI corruption case, the Madras High Court judgment delivered on 4-12-2001 acquitted her of the sentence. Reporters met me then and asked: “In that case the AIADMK Government itself is the prosecution. They themselves are the accused. In that situation they have been acquitted. Will the State Government therefore appeal the judgment to the Supreme Court?” I replied: “We are people who respect justice. I respect justice even more than a judge respects justice.”

When reporters again asked whether I thought justice would prevail if the matter went to the Supreme Court, I said: “How can that be said in advance? Some people may be able to say in which court justice will be obtained; I cannot.” Later, when the Supreme Court delivered its judgment, I remembered that first comment of mine.

When news came that Jayalalithaa had been acquitted in the Madras High Court, a major question arose before many people: how had the AIADMK member who had won Andipatti resigned his seat a few days earlier, apparently anticipating that such a judgment would come?

On 5-12-2001 itself, the New Delhi edition of the *Indian Express* carried an editorial. Important portions, in the source’s Tamil rendering, were as follows:

“By acquitting AIADMK leader Jayalalithaa in the TANSI and Pleasant Stay Hotel cases, the Madras High Court has cleared the way for her to become Chief Minister again. All the charges stated in the Special Court were not accepted by the High Court on appeal, and she was held not guilty and acquitted. Because of that, Jayalalithaa and her supporters are saying that it has been proved that the case was one imposed upon her. That too is not acceptable. The reason is that the lower court had already upheld the charge. There is no guarantee that Justice Dinakar’s judgment will agree with a judgment given by a High Court Bench or the Supreme Court. Even the celebrations that arose around the judgment announced on Tuesday were unnecessary. This judgment will not in any way alter the political situation in the State.

“Even if Jayalalithaa does not herself formally hold power there, it is she who is wholly running the administration in Tamil Nadu. The entire power of government has been wasted because of personal political vendetta and because of a regime of illicit-liquor traders and rowdies. They are the ones who are effective in Tamil Nadu today. When influential or wealthy people stand in the dock, it is not very easy to prove them guilty. Because witnesses turn hostile, charges against important people in public life are not proved. In that sense, saying that Jayalalithaa is innocent has raised many eyebrows.”

Udanpirappē,

Jayalalithaa claims in her statement that she was acquitted in twelve cases filed against her. In this TANSI case too she was acquitted. But what did Supreme Court judges say in that very case on 24-11-2003?

“Although we have reached the conclusion that Jayalalithaa is not guilty, it is clearly seen that lands belonging to the public sector were sold to companies in which Jayalalithaa was a partner while she held office as Chief Minister. Once the Chief Minister herself sought to purchase the properties, we are able to see that the bureaucracy showed excessive interest and smoothly completed the sale at the very price desired by Chief Minister Jayalalithaa.

“Jayalalithaa’s actions are contrary to the substance and spirit of the rules of conduct. To speak fairly, can there be one law for minor government officials and another law for the Chief Minister? In such situations, is a rule of good conduct merely an ornamental object to be placed in a museum? Is it not something that must be followed? These are the things that trouble our conscience.

“The first accused, Jayalalithaa, has gone so far, in order to escape from this case, as to deny even that the signature on the TANSI land deed is hers. Therefore Jayalalithaa must unconditionally return the properties and, on the basis of what we have said, come forward to answer to her own conscience.”

It is these observations by the judges that Jayalalithaa now describes by saying that the court acquitted her in the case against her. Do not the very words of the Supreme Court judges show us how she was acquitted?

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**31-5-2010**
