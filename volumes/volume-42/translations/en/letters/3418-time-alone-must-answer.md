# 3418. Time Alone Must Answer!

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This letter is a political and legal rebuttal concerning the Mullaperiyar dam dispute and earlier court decisions involving Jayalalithaa. Judicial events, statutes, newspaper editorials and attributed statements are translated only as presented in the 2009 source. No current legal conclusion or independent adjudication is added. The complete audited Tamil is reproduced below and remains authoritative.

**Source pages:** [PDF 344](../../../pages/page-344.md)–[PDF 351](../../../pages/page-351.md)  
**Date:** 13 October 2009

**Udanpirappē,**

Newspapers have reported that the Supreme Court rejected a petition filed on behalf of the Tamil Nadu government seeking a stay on the Union Government’s permission to the Kerala government to carry out survey work for constructing a new dam in the Mullaperiyar dam area, and adjourned the case until 20-10-2009.

A statement then said:

> “There is nothing surprising in the Supreme Court issuing an order as though it had slapped Karunanidhi in the face.”

The author points out that this statement was not issued by the Chief Minister of Kerala or by any Kerala minister.

It was issued by Tamil Nadu Opposition Leader Jayalalithaa.

When the Tamil Nadu government argues for the welfare of Tamil Nadu’s people and for Tamil Nadu’s rights, if it does not immediately receive a favourable order, the author says, journalists and politicians in Tamil Nadu should consider what it means for someone to rejoice and issue such a statement.

A case had already been filed by the Tamil Nadu government in the Supreme Court concerning the construction of a new dam in the Mullaperiyar area, and its hearing had been fixed for 20-10-2009.

In the fresh petition seeking a stay on the Union Government’s permission, the Supreme Court, according to the author, merely observed that because the main case itself was coming up for hearing on the 20th of that month, the new matter could be heard along with it then.

Yet, he says, Jayalalithaa celebrated this as an order that had “slapped” him in the face.

If this is to be called a slap in the face, he says, let them decide what names should be given to the following judgments.

In the 2001 general election, Jayalalithaa filed nomination papers in four constituencies: **Krishnagiri, Andipatti, Bhuvanagiri and Pudukkottai**.

During scrutiny, Krishnagiri Returning Officer Mathivanan rejected her nomination on the grounds, the source says, that she had been sentenced to more than two years’ imprisonment and had also filed nominations in more than two constituencies.

The letter says Jayalalithaa had been sentenced to three years’ imprisonment in the TANSI land-deal case.

It states that Section 8 of the Representation of the People Act provided that a person sentenced to imprisonment for more than two years could not contest an election.

The author then says that once a court convicted a person and sentenced that person to not less than two years’ imprisonment, the person became disqualified from contesting elections from the date of conviction.

Former West Bengal Chief Minister and prominent lawyer Siddhartha Shankar Ray appeared for Jayalalithaa and argued, according to the source, that because the High Court had stayed the prison sentence, her nomination should be accepted.

But the Returning Officer rejected the nomination on the basis of the Representation of the People Act and a 1997 Election Commission circular.

Regarding the rejection of Jayalalithaa’s nominations, the author quotes the *Express* of 26 April 2001 as writing, in substance:

> There was nothing surprising about the rejection of all four nomination papers filed by AIADMK leader Jayalalithaa.
>
> The election officers had strictly followed the Election Commission’s 1997 circular that a person sentenced to more than two years could not contest.
>
> Although the law said a candidate should not file nominations in more than two constituencies, Jayalalithaa tried her luck in four.
>
> Jayalalithaa, who did not respect public opinion, had calculated that she could come to power on a sympathy wave generated by the rejection of her nominations.

The source then says *The Hindu* wrote:

> As expected, Jayalalithaa has been denied permission to contest the election.
>
> Once the Election Commission confirmed its 1997 order regarding disqualification, Jayalalithaa’s disqualification became certain.
>
> If she thought that contesting from four places might give her a chance to contest from at least one, that was an incorrect calculation and itself became another reason confirming her disqualification.

The 6-5-2001 issue of *Ananda Vikatan*, the author says, wrote in its editorial:

> Jayalalithaa’s approach in this election has gone beyond norms of justice and propriety.
>
> Even after her nominations were rejected, and even after she had lost eligibility to be elected as a Member of the Legislative Assembly, she did not appear to change her plan.
>
> She was proclaiming wherever she went that if the AIADMK alliance won, she herself would be Chief Minister.
>
> It was painful, the editorial said, that she was willing to treat people as fools on the assumption that they did not know political conventions and that only by projecting herself as Chief Minister would they vote.
>
> Had she, after being barred from contesting, fully accepted the decision and said, “Make our alliance win. If you do, I will install as Chief Minister a leader acceptable to you on my behalf and strive to provide good government,” that would have been acceptable.

But, the author continues, while election results were still coming in on the 14th, a meeting of AIADMK legislators was hurriedly convened.

Jayalalithaa was elected leader of the Legislature Party.

That very evening, Governor Fathima Beevi administered the oath of office to her.

Newspapers at the time, the author says, reported that she was sworn in so hurriedly because of fear that someone might file a case seeking to prevent her from assuming office.

In litigation before the Supreme Court challenging her assumption of office as Chief Minister, the source says Justices K. T. Thomas and S. N. Variava issued a significant ruling on 2-8-2001.

The letter renders their position as follows:

> A person convicted in a corruption case should not continue in public office until found innocent by a court.
>
> An appellate court should not stay the lower court’s finding of guilt; it may, if appropriate, temporarily stay the sentence.
>
> But a person convicted of corruption should not continue in public office until cleared of the charges by a court, and the courts should not assist such a person in occupying office.

In the case challenging the legality of Jayalalithaa’s appointment as Chief Minister, the source says a five-judge bench headed by Justice S. P. Bharucha delivered a unanimous judgment on 21-9-2001:

> Jayalalithaa’s appointment as Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu was invalid.
>
> The oath administered to her on 14 May by then Governor Fathima Beevi was contrary to the Constitution.
>
> She could no longer continue as Chief Minister because her appointment on 14 May was not legally valid.

When her lawyer K. K. Venugopal asked the judges for a few days to make alternative arrangements, the source says the Court replied:

> “When a Chief Minister dies, an alternative arrangement is made immediately. A party with such a large majority can elect a new leader at any time.”

The request was therefore rejected.

★★

The author then gives another example.

During Jayalalithaa’s government, the Registrar of the High Court itself filed a case seeking the filling of vacant posts of judges and other judicial staff.

The case came before a bench comprising Chief Justice Subhashan Reddy and Justice Raviraja Pandian on 6-3-2003.

The Tamil Nadu government said that the posts had not been filled because of shortage of funds.

The source reproduces the judges’ criticism as follows:

> “How can courts function without judges and staff?
>
> The Court has grown tired of writing to the Tamil Nadu government asking it to fill vacant posts.
>
> The government is not doing charity to the judiciary.
>
> Under the Constitution, it has a duty to allocate the necessary funds.
>
> If the government does not provide adequate funds to the judiciary, the courts may have to be closed.
>
> If this situation continues, a recommendation will be made to the constitutional authority responsible for safeguarding the law that Tamil Nadu be declared a State affected by financial emergency.
>
> Is this the respect the Tamil Nadu government gives the High Court?”

About that judicial criticism, the source says the weekly *Kalki* wrote in its editorial dated 23-3-2003:

> Madras High Court Chief Justice Subhashan Reddy had strongly criticised the Tamil Nadu government and said it might become necessary to recommend to the Union Government that a state of financial emergency be declared in Tamil Nadu.
>
> He criticised the government for not appointing enough court employees and for not allocating adequate funds to develop the basic needs of courts.
>
> At the same time, the Tamil Nadu government was announcing new welfare works and schemes district after district and publishing full-page newspaper advertisements carrying a life-size image of the Chief Minister.
>
> Chief Minister Jayalalithaa, who began her administration saying the treasury was empty and announced staff reductions and bonus cuts on a broad scale, was travelling rapidly to inaugurate those schemes and using the stages to describe both her achievements and the troubles, as she portrayed them, caused by opposition parties.
>
> The editorial said there was no limit to the lavish expenditure on the functions and on the Chief Minister’s travel.

★★

The author adds another example.

Was the judgment in the case concerning Jayalalithaa’s purchase of TANSI land an ordinary one, he asks?

Did the Supreme Court not direct that the land be returned and that Jayalalithaa act according to her conscience?

When Jayalalithaa now describes a ten-day adjournment by the Supreme Court as a judgment that slapped Karunanidhi in the face, the author says, she herself must say where the earlier judgments delivered during her period in office should be described as having struck.

Jayalalithaa also cited a Kerala minister’s statement that survey work had begun at the start of 2007, and alleged that I knew this information but concealed it when filing the case in the Supreme Court.

The author asks: if what the Kerala minister and Jayalalithaa say is true, why was no such survey mentioned in the counter-affidavit filed by the Kerala government on **30-3-2007**, or in the affidavits filed by its five witnesses in 2008, or during cross-examination?

In reality, he says, Kerala gave Tamil Nadu no information about survey work.

It did not even inform Tamil Nadu that it had sought permission from the Union Government to conduct a survey.

The area where Kerala is said to have carried out the survey falls within a wildlife sanctuary.

Under the Wildlife Protection Act, prior permission is required before any such survey work can begin there.

Further, under the Forest Conservation Act, the source says, prior permission must also be obtained through the Supreme Court.

Is Jayalalithaa saying, the author asks, that Kerala conducted the survey without any such permission?

If it is claimed that the survey had already taken place with permission, was Union Minister of State Jairam Ramesh wrong when he said permission had been granted only on the 6th of the current month?

The author says there was no information that Kerala had obtained any permission in 2007 or earlier.

Those in Tamil Nadu who seek the welfare of Tamils and the prosperity of Tamil Nadu, he says, may be driven to ask whether merely going to courts is enough, or whether decisions must ultimately be made in the people’s forum.

**Time alone must answer.**

He adds one further point.

It was while Jayalalithaa was Chief Minister, on **18-3-2006**, that the Kerala government enacted a law in its Legislative Assembly that created a situation in which the Supreme Court’s earlier judgment was not implemented.

The author says his side did not, merely because that act was against Jayalalithaa’s government, immediately welcome it or issue a statement like hers.

The case filed in the Supreme Court by the Jayalalithaa government against Kerala’s law was continued by the DMK government after it took office in May 2006.

That, he says, shows that his side’s concern is the welfare of Tamil Nadu and the Tamil people, rather than the identity of whoever happens to be in office.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**13-10-2009**
