# 3549. Come! Come! O Ranks of Striped Tigers, Come!

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil is reproduced in full below the translation and remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.” Literally evoking “one born alongside me,” Kalaignar uses it as a distinctive address of shared identity, equality, affection, and solidarity within the movement.

**Source pages:** [PDF 119](../../../pages/page-119.md)–[PDF 122](../../../pages/page-122.md)  
**Date:** 22 March 2011

**Udanpirappē,**

The camps of the various parties preparing to enter the election field have been set up and are almost ready. I look closely at the chariots, horses, men and great army of the alliance standing in the opposing ranks. As soon as I understood the reason Indrajit could not be seen in those opposing ranks, I reflected on it for a few moments—but, realising that allowing that thought a place on the battlefield would disturb our aim, I did not worry about it further.

The Dravidian consciousness, with a history of more than 5,000 years, had been kept from withering in the great form called Periyar. Then arrows of division entered and we became two movements. Of those, one movement functioned under Anna as a movement of ethnic consciousness and rationalism; the other, without concern for rationalism but preserving culture, functioned under the leadership of my dear friend M. G. R. Seeing that flourishing condition, how many conspiracies and manoeuvres were launched to diminish its beauty!

Enduring all of them, alternately defeating one another and falling because of one another, the two Dravidian movements grew and prospered into forces capable of governing the country. Seeing this, an army of mythic orthodoxy gathered to destroy them both. A dove of reconciliation flew down from the north, saying, “Let the two armies become one army; come, let us form ranks to defeat the fierce common enemy.” The conspirators broke even that dove's wings and drove it away. Still there was no peace. Some wanted the fratricidal war to continue, thinking that only then would their mouths be filled with sugar. Because they entered into that dishonourable enterprise, spiders began spinning webs so that the movement created by the strong Dravidian leaders might break apart and scatter.

The movement that did not get caught in that web is this movement of valiant people created by Anna—the DMK. That is why the web has not torn it apart, nor has it gone for a price. With integrity that cannot be shaken, justice, righteousness, truth, firmness and sincerity, it stands at the front of the formation as a movement strengthened by all these, raising the sword of righteous struggle.

Whenever any movement enters the field to face an election on the democratic path, it is natural that not only the general public—who must safeguard democracy—but also the volunteers and comrades who gather support among that public for their respective parties will assemble in organised ranks. Whatever ranks they belong to, let us honour and praise them as people of our own ranks. Even when circumstances place them and us in opposing formations, if we work with the thought that we have all joined this movement of democracy to carry out—to complete—the sacred work of democracy's temple and to rejoice in its beauty and rise, then we will have dedicated ourselves to preserving dignity, honouring humaneness and increasing the glory of the soil in which we were born.

We must celebrate the victory won through our sweat. We must not, through our own weariness, let that victory slip away and push ourselves into a condition where democratic dignity itself is attacked. That is why, just as chaff is winnowed away while the wind is blowing and the useful grains are gathered and kept, we have arranged the warriors of our army in formation.

An army that knows no weariness—  
an army that never comes up short—  
even if heaven calls, “Come!”,  
this is not a false army that,  
dazzled by it, says one thing  
and does another,  
pretending to wage war;

“If this army is defeated, which army can win?”  
our Anna would often say.  
That very army is this army!

Had we remained without separating from Periyar, the victory this army would achieve would be a Himalayan victory—a victory that could bend the Himalayas.

Even so, Anna—who said, “The chair in which my leader sat remains empty,” and who remained Periyar's volunteer, his disciple and the voice of his principles—united all of us, placed the sword of battle in our hands and sent us forth saying, “Win the democratic righteous struggle, younger brothers!” Although one or two of those younger brothers scattered here and there, this is the hand that wandered everywhere and struggled hard to unite them again.

This is the hour when, bound by the feeling embraced by this hand, I call: come, all of you, let us unite and face the field together!

Let us wipe away the condition that makes our enemies jump with delight saying that Dravidians are a sack of gooseberries.

Those who left—  
those who were driven away—  
those who were chased away—

Come, beating your broad shoulders and declaring: “Our valiant breath, fragrant in red blood, will not be sold—this is Tamil breath!” I wait in penance like ascetics with closed eyes, like pure sages, calling you to come and wear the garland of victory—not because the strength of the army is insufficient, but because the army we have must become still stronger, firmer, and certain to wear the garland of victory!

My beloved Udanpirappus!  
Those dearer to me than my own life!  
Together, let us all taste the joyous outcome!  
Come! Come! O ranks of striped tigers, come!

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**22-3-2011**

## Letter-specific notes

1. The English preserves the source's Indrajit, dove, spider-web, chaff-and-grain, Himalayan, gooseberry-sack and army imagery without adding outside mythological or political identifications.
2. The source's wordplay around `வலை` (web/net) and `விலை` (price) is represented conservatively rather than expanded into an explanatory claim.
3. The verse-like passages and Anna quotation are kept as distinct lines to preserve the source's rhetorical form.
