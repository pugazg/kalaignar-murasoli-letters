# 3779. The Height of Danger: Foreign Direct Investment!

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil is reproduced in full below the translation and remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.” Literally evoking “one born alongside me,” Kalaignar uses it as a distinctive address of shared identity, equality, affection, and solidarity within the movement.

**Udanpirappē,**

In the letter I wrote last year, I explained that permitting foreign companies to make direct investments in retail trade would simultaneously harm lakhs of small retailers and crores of ordinary poor and middle-class people who purchase and use goods as consumers. I said it was dangerous and could become one of the root causes of the country’s economic decline. I also said that the Union Government could in no way be justified in compelling State Governments to accept that decision.

In my statement of 28 November 2012, I asked the Union Government not to insist that its decision permitting foreign investment in retail trade be implemented in the States—not merely to refrain from insisting, but not even to offer it as advice. I asked it to respect the views of parties including the DMK and the united demand of the trading community, and withdraw the decision immediately.

The Union Government, which opened the way for foreign direct investment of up to one hundred per cent in several sectors including retail trade, has now granted increased permission for FDI even in two sectors vital to India’s security and administrative secrets: telecommunications and defence-equipment production.

The decision was taken at a meeting of the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs chaired by the Prime Minister on 16 July 2013. The existing FDI ceiling of seventy-four per cent in telecommunications has been raised to one hundred per cent. Companies may obtain investment up to forty-nine per cent without prior Government approval.

Although the existing ceiling of twenty-six per cent FDI in defence is formally to continue, the Cabinet Committee on Security may approve individual projects involving advanced manufacturing technology. The FDI limit in insurance is to be raised from twenty-six to forty-nine per cent. The existing forty-nine per cent ceiling in civil aviation remains. FDI in construction companies is to be increased from forty-nine to one hundred per cent. In single-brand retail, the existing forty-nine per cent foreign-investment limit is to be raised to one hundred per cent. Altogether, decisions have been taken to raise FDI limits in twelve sectors.

Because the telecommunications limit is being raised from seventy-four to one hundred per cent, some large foreign companies that have so far operated jointly with Indian companies may now say that they will conduct the business entirely on their own capital. They will no longer need the cooperation of Indian companies. Indian firms that have operated with foreign backing may be unable to compete independently. The fate of Indian companies operating with limited capital may become one of utter helplessness. The welfare of several lakhs of employees may be abandoned in midstream.

While joint ventures of foreign and Indian companies in telecommunications are currently earning profits, the unaudited accounts of the Government of India’s BSNL show a different condition: against a profit of ₹525 crore in 2008–09, there was a loss of ₹1,823 crore in 2009–10; ₹6,384 crore in 2010–11; ₹8,851 crore in 2011–12; and ₹8,198 crore in 2012–13. Yet our Union Government has opened further avenues for foreign companies to earn still greater profits.

The BSNL National Federation has strongly opposed the Government of India’s decision to permit one hundred per cent FDI in telecommunications. It charges that the Union Government is acting according to the wishes of Western countries. There is considerable justice in its view that Government public-sector companies BSNL and MTNL will be directly harmed.

Employees and officers in the insurance sector have launched protests against raising the FDI ceiling to forty-nine per cent. They say that, although the limit cannot be raised without Parliament’s approval, the Union Government is attempting to arrange it through a shortcut. The Parliamentary Standing Committee has already said that the existing twenty-six per cent foreign-investment allowance in insurance need not be increased. If that recommendation is ignored and the limit is nevertheless raised to forty-nine per cent, the question cannot be avoided: what then is the purpose of a Parliamentary Standing Committee?

Countries such as the United States, France and Canada do not permit more than thirty-five per cent foreign direct investment in telecommunications. Why should India alone raise it to this extent? If foreign companies are allowed such dominance in telecommunications, should we not consider whether that is compatible with national security? The concern cannot be dismissed. Even after reports that the United States uses its intelligence agencies and internet communications to spy on countries such as India, it is impossible to imagine where a decision of this kind will lead.

The truth is that the Government of India has felt compelled to permit greater foreign investment in order to control the fall in the rupee against the US dollar and reduce the country’s growing current-account deficit.

Announcing the decisions, Union Commerce and Industry Minister Anand Sharma told journalists that they had been taken to address the “concerns” of foreign investors. Does the Government of India care only about the “concerns” of foreign investors? Does it have no “concern” for domestic investors or Government public-sector enterprises? That question cannot be prevented from arising.

These decisions are justified as medicines needed to cure the illness in India’s economy and arrest the rupee’s decline. One must act, as the *Tirukkural* says, by “examining the disease, examining its root cause, and examining the means by which it may be cured.” Medicine given for a disease must alleviate it, reduce it and cure it fully. But the medicines discovered by the Union Government will not alleviate the illness; they may create side effects and multiply the damage. That warning must be taken seriously.

With a general election to Parliament due next year, it must also be considered whether the Union Government should take decisions this severe. They are capable of producing grave consequences. In particular, they open the way for the country’s defence-equipment production to be brought under foreign control.

The Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam wholly opposes the Union Government’s decisions to increase foreign direct investment in these important sectors.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**21 July 2013**
