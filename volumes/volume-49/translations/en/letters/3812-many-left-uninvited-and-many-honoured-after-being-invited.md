# 3812. Many Left Uninvited—and Many “Honoured” after Being Invited?

> **Translator’s note**

**Date:** 28 September 2013

**Udanpirappē,**

I wish to offer a few observations about the centenary celebration of Indian cinema—an event that became an example not of how a celebration ought to be conducted, but of how it ought not to be conducted. This letter is not merely the product of my personal anguish. It brings together the anger and pain that arose in the hearts of many artists in the film industry, along with—most notably and rather surprisingly—the views published by a number of Tamil journals.

The Government of Tamil Nadu and the South Indian Film Chamber of Commerce jointly conducted a four-day celebration marking one hundred years of Indian cinema. At the concluding function on the 24th, the President of India, Pranab Mukherjee, attended and presented the first award to Jayalalithaa as the “foremost achiever” who had made a great contribution to the growth of Indian cinema over the past century. After praising her highly, he distributed awards to others and departed.

Many people have referred to me in connection with this celebration and written about it in various journals. Many reporters who met me asked for my views. A correspondent from a senior weekly—one bearing the name of a national leader—telephoned me and asked: “So many wrongs have occurred. Why do you keep saying that you will say nothing about them?” I am obliged to express my gratitude to all of them.

Although I did not wish to speak about this cinema celebration, many people strongly urged me to record my view. I therefore set out only the reports I have read and heard. This is not written to accuse anyone or to search for fault. When functions of this kind are organised by a Government or an institution with Government assistance drawn from the taxpayers’ money, it is essential that they be conducted in such a manner as to leave not even the smallest opening for criticism.

Newspapers and other media have reported that Karnataka’s Minister for Culture condemned the treatment of film artists who travelled from Karnataka to Tamil Nadu for the celebration, saying that they were made to wander without proper accommodation, transport facilities or necessary permissions, and demanding that the Tamil Nadu Chief Minister accept responsibility and apologise.

More particularly, who presided over the concluding function held on the 24th? Whether a celebration is conducted by the Government or by a private organisation, the long-established convention is that one person presides. But in the full-page advertisements for this function, three people were named under the description “those presiding”: Tamil Nadu Governor Dr K. Rosaiah, Tamil Nadu Chief Minister Selvi J. Jayalalithaa and Kerala Chief Minister Thiru Oommen Chandy. Thus, three people appear to have presided over one function.

When the President, a Governor and Chief Ministers take part in an event of this kind, proper protocol is to describe the President as the chief guest, the Governor as the person presiding, and the Chief Ministers as those gracing or leading the occasion. Why, then, were both the Governor and the Chief Ministers announced as presiding over this event? You may understand the reason yourselves. The organisers must explain how many presidents a single function can have.

Nor is that all. At this four-day celebration, veteran artists such as Superstar Rajinikanth, Kalaignani Kamal Haasan, Isaignani Ilaiyaraaja and Ilatchiya Nadigar S. S. Rajendran were not allotted seats in the front row. Worse still, after they had gone and seated themselves in the front row, they were made to rise and move to the rows behind. This uncivilised act humiliated the entire artistic community.

Even if the organisers had not originally assigned those accomplished artists front-row seats, they should have received them upon arrival and guided them to places specifically reserved for them. Instead, removing them after they had taken their seats and making them sit behind was plainly an act devoid of culture.

Awards were then presented to veteran artists, photographs were taken with the Chief Minister, and those photographs were published in the newspapers. In one such photograph I saw Rajini and Kamal standing somewhere in the second row. How much pain would their lakhs of admirers have felt on seeing those pictures? I repeat: I do not say this merely because I wish to find fault.

The Chief Minister and other ministers may not have had the time to attend to each of these details. The organisers should have considered them carefully. Artists are generally people of intense self-respect, as delicate as the *anicham* flower. They must not be treated in a way that wounds their minds or injures their dignity.

Next, invitations to this celebration were not sent to most members of the press—the institution proudly called democracy’s “Fourth Estate.” Many television channels were not even permitted to enter. Poor things! They publish news in so many ways supportive of this Government. Should they not at least have been rewarded with an invitation? Journalists from weekly magazines entered as ordinary “spectators,” gathered information and published reports.

As I have already told reporters, daily newspapers under the AIADMK Government—some moved by “benefit,” others by “fear”—did not come forward to publish such details about the cinema celebration and concealed them from public attention. Yet a few weeklies have written “courageously” about them over the past two or three days.

In *Kumudam Reporter*, journalist R. Murugesan wrote a three-page article about the celebration. Some important portions read as follows:

> Actor Rajinikanth, who received an award at the Indian Cinema Centenary Celebration, stood apart when the group photograph with the Chief Minister was taken. Actor Vijay, who had been caught in the controversy over the film *Thalaivaa*, had arrived early, yet went and sat in the back row. None of the actors and actresses who came after him sat beside him; they left several seats empty and sat farther away. Only selected journalists and photographers were admitted to the function.
>
> Rajinikanth arrived with his daughter Soundarya. Fans applauded, whistled and cheered. Rajini went towards the stage, waved to the fans, offered his greetings and sat in the front row. Government officials then told him, “According to protocol, a seat has been allotted to you in the third row,” and sent him there. What kind of “protocol” this was must be explained by the organisers.
>
> Below the stage were seated Sasikala and all the ministers. Ordinarily, at a cinema function, Rajini and Kamal would sit to the right and left of the Chief Minister’s seat. At this event, film producers sat close to the Chief Minister; actresses including Manorama, Saroja Devi and L. R. Eswari sat in the second row; and Rajini and Kamal sat in the third row. For the group photograph, Kamal and Ilaiyaraaja were seated in chairs. Government officials asked them to stand up. In the group photograph Rajini, for some reason, stood apart at the extreme left.

That is the substance of the report in *Kumudam Reporter*.

The cover of yesterday’s *Junior Vikatan* described the function as: “A Cinema Celebration Turned into a Sycophancy Festival—Rajini Last, Kamal Embittered, Vijay Marginalised.” It carried their photographs and a lengthy article.

Journalists K. Rajiv Gandhi and C. Kaverimanickam compiled that article. Some portions read:

> The “Cinema 100” celebration, which should have been recorded and celebrated in history, ended as though it were an AIADMK party function. After controversy arose over Karunanidhi not being invited, an invitation was delivered to him only a few hours before the event began.
>
> It is said that the Chief Minister personally selected all the films screened in Chennai theatres for the centenary. Even films such as *Paruthiveeran* and *Aravaan* were placed among the important works, while films such as *Parasakthi*, *Manthiri Kumari* and *Veerapandiya Kattabomman*—which produced a revolution in Tamil cinema dialogue—found no place in the list.
>
> Because the Tamil Nadu Government gave ten crore rupees in financial assistance, the broadcasting rights went entirely to Jaya TV. Other channels were therefore denied entry. Journalists were not issued identity cards until the last moment. Many people entered the auditorium without either invitations or entry passes. Several AIADMK members intimidated the security staff, saying: “Don’t you know who I am? Are you asking an AIADMK man for an identity card?” and forced their way inside.
>
> Everyone from the person who gave the welcome address to the person who proposed the vote of thanks sang Jayalalithaa’s praises. As though that were not enough, even the cultural performances praised her. Jayalalithaa proudly declared that Tamil cinema had functioned freely for the past two years. Kamal and Vijay sat listening to it all.
>
> Rajini and Kamal were among those receiving awards. Rajini sat at the very end of the last row on the stage. He was called only after awards had been given to thirty people. The moment his name was announced, he came forward quickly. He received the award and left just as quickly. Jayalalithaa showed no reaction. When Kamal came to receive his award, not even a faint smile appeared on her face. Jayalalithaa was not in the hall when Rajini and Kamal spoke.
>
> The highlight of the event was the arrival of Vijay, who had reportedly not been invited. He was not given an award. In a video prepared by the Film Chamber, Karunanidhi’s photograph and name appeared for a few seconds. Clips from two Vijayakanth films were shown, but his face was carefully avoided.
>
> The number of prominent personalities who did not attend was greater than the number who came. Balu Mahendra, Bharathiraja, Goundamani, Senthil, Vadivelu, S. P. Balasubrahmanyam, Shankar, Mani Ratnam, P. Susheela, S. Janaki and many others who made major contributions to Tamil cinema were absent. It is said that many of them were not invited at all. Balu Mahendra attended only the following day.
>
> Sridevi attended, but was not given an award. Even the name of A. R. Rahman, winner of two Academy Awards, was absent from the awards list. People say the reason was that he composed the anthem for the Classical Tamil Conference. Film personalities lament: “If Jayalalithaa wanted to honour only the film people she liked, she could have held a separate function. Why did they turn a common celebration called Cinema 100 into this kind of sycophancy festival?”

Alongside this report, in its “Kazhugar Answers” section, *Junior Vikatan* was asked: “Was it not wrong to exclude Karunanidhi from the film industry’s centenary celebration?” It answered:

> Certainly. When Karunanidhi was asked, “Indian cinema has reached the age of one hundred. Were you invited to the celebration?” he replied: “Who am I, for them to invite me?” Within those four words lies seventy years of cinema.
>
> During his schooldays, Karunanidhi adapted *Kundalakesi*, one of the five great Tamil epics, into a short play and sent it to the Tiruchi radio station. They returned it, saying it could not be broadcast. That play later became the film *Manthiri Kumari*. Through that film Tamil Nadu gained M. G. R.; M. G. R. gained a Janaki. It was through the dialogue of *Parasakthi* that Tamil cinema gained Sivaji.
>
> When Karunanidhi wrote dialogue for *Rajakumari*, India had not yet attained independence. The last film for which he wrote story and dialogue was *Ponnar–Shankar*, released two years ago. So far, seventy-five films have been made from Karunanidhi’s stories and screenplays. He wrote songs for twenty-one films, including *Manthiri Kumari*, *Parasakthi*, *Poompuhar* and *Poomaalai*. “Kāgitha Odam Kadalalai Mīthu” is still heard as a night-time song.
>
> Karunanidhi ran four production companies: Mekala Pictures, Anjugam Pictures, Kalaiezhil Combines and Poompuhar Productions. From *Naam* to *Paasakiligal*, he produced about twenty-nine films. *Kanchi Thalaivan*, which crowned M. G. R., and *Poompuhar*, which established S. S. Rajendran, were his productions. It was his company that made Jayalalithaa “Engal Thangam.” Surely a man with such a record should have been invited to the celebration.

*Nakkheeran* also compiled and published the same developments. Its article concluded:

> They said that they were proudly celebrating one hundred years of Indian cinema. Yet the function was not conducted in a way that enabled those who brought honour to cinema to feel proud or happy. The artists who gave cinema its distinction were not joyful at this centenary. Everyone is seething with the feeling that they were humiliated.

It has spread through Kollywood that Rajini told a few close friends:

> I should not have attended the function. I went only because the Film Chamber invited me. They humiliated me very badly. Every artist was humiliated at that function.

*Nakkheeran* further wrote:

> In the end, the centenary celebration that should have taken place with the joy of Deepavali became a function conducted as though it were meant for Jaya TV’s Deepavali broadcast. This resentment is echoing throughout the cinema industry.

Under the headline “Cinema 100—Problems 1000,” *Ananda Vikatan* published a long article by reporters writing as the “Vikatan Team.” I shall mention only a few passages:

> Amid many confusions and muddles, the Cinema 100 celebration was staged. An event supposedly organised to honour cinema personalities wounded many prominent people who had brought distinction to Tamil cinema for half a century.
>
> Karunanidhi, former Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu, who made his presence emphatically felt in Tamil cinema through films such as *Parasakthi* and *Manohara*, received an invitation only on the day before the celebration. Even that was a formality delivered in the tone of: “Please do not attend.” Vijayakanth, who had served as President of the Nadigar Sangam, was not invited at all.
>
> Director Mahendran was not invited. Director S. P. Muthuraman was not invited. It is said that the Government side issued strict orders that Vijayakanth, Parthiban, Karthik, Radhika and Vadivelu should not attend. M. S. Viswanathan, Panchu Arunachalam, Cho, Bharathiraja, Goundamani, Vairamuthu, Mani Ratnam, Shankar, A. R. Murugadoss, A. R. Rahman, Vijayakumar and Film News Anandan received neither awards nor invitations.
>
> Film Chamber administrators are said to have ordered: “This is Amma’s function. Heroes and heroines must attend. Strict action will be taken against those who do not.”

The principal special guest at this function, conducted at the cost of many crores of rupees, was President of India Pranab Mukherjee. I do not know of any newspaper that published his speech in full. His participation appears to have been treated merely as “he too attended.”

Those who sent Superstar Rajinikanth to the third row “according to protocol” issued a full-page advertisement on 20 September 2013 in which the Chief Minister’s name appeared first in large bold letters. Poor President of India—his name appeared last. The advertisement carried a full-length image of Jayalalithaa, but no photograph of the President, the Governor or the Chief Ministers of the other States. Whether this was proper “according to protocol” is something the President’s Secretariat must explain.

Anyone familiar with the evolution of Tamil cinema, on seeing the strange happenings at this celebration jointly conducted by the AIADMK Government, would remember the lines written by Pattukkottai Kalyanasundaram in 1959 for the film *Mahadevi*, starring Puratchi Nadigar M. G. R.:

> Building walls out of intrigue,  
> raising forts out of selfishness,  
> casting aside the justice that shone,  
> saying, “No witnesses are needed;  
> I alone am everything”—  
> is this the world of power  
> where worthless men dance their tricks?

The weeklies have written not only about my not being invited, but also about many incidents at the function. Reading them, I am worried about what may now happen to those journals. Yet when I read how some of the great artists who were invited were treated, I can only say: “Fortunately they did not invite us. At least our self-respect was saved.”

The Indian Cinema Centenary Celebration thus “honoured” many by leaving them uninvited—and “honoured” many others after inviting them!

**With affection,**  
**M. K.**
28 September 2013
