# 3689. The Humiliation—the Question Is: By Whom?

> **Translator’s note**

## English translation

Udanpirappē,

“Jayalalithaa walks out”—that is today's headline and major story in every newspaper. For a week it had been publicised that the Tamil Nadu Chief Minister was going to Delhi for the National Development Council meeting. After attending yesterday, she alleged that she was denied permission to speak beyond ten minutes and said the Union Government had thereby humiliated the people of Tamil Nadu. When reporters excitedly brought me the news yesterday, I said that if the Centre had humiliated Jayalalithaa or Tamil Nadu I condemned it, but that I first wanted to know fully what had happened.

At National Development Council meetings and Chief Ministers' conferences, speakers are generally called in alphabetical order, though seniority by age is sometimes given preference—as happened to me when I was Chief Minister. Yesterday, since Tamil Nadu comes under the English letter T and she would otherwise speak late, the Chief Minister requested to speak first because she needed to return immediately to Chennai. That request was accepted, and after the Prime Minister's opening address she was the first Chief Minister called.

She told reporters that a bell rang after ten minutes and that this humiliated both her and Tamil Nadu. More than thirty Chief Ministers and Central Ministers—around forty speakers—had to speak. Even ten minutes each means 400 minutes, more than six hours. Everyone had therefore been informed beforehand that they should finish within ten minutes.

If she wanted more time, she could have requested it in advance. Or she could have spoken only the important parts within ten minutes, asked that the remainder be treated as laid on the proceedings, and distributed the full text to the press. A bell did not compel her to stop that instant; she could have continued for a few more moments and concluded. No one would have prevented her. Instead, because of her characteristic quick temper, she hurried out, accused the Centre of humiliating Tamil Nadu and flew back to Chennai.

Ringing a bell to indicate that a speech should conclude is not humiliating. The Tamil Nadu Assembly itself has a bell. Members often continue speaking after it rings. During grant debates, the Speaker may tell each member to finish within five minutes because many must speak before the Chief Minister replies. That is normal procedure. Yet the Chief Minister compared this to ringing a school bell to make children sit down. Does that mean the Assembly bell is also like a school bell?

When she recently held a District Collectors' conference, what would happen if every Collector spoke for half an hour or forty-five minutes listing everything the State Government had failed to do for the district? When would the conference end?

In her own interview Jayalalithaa acknowledged that she had been told beforehand that each Chief Minister would receive ten minutes and that a bell would ring at the end. Why then did she not object before beginning, ask for more time, or request that no bell be rung while Chief Ministers spoke?

The meeting was to finalise the draft Twelfth Five-Year Plan. Ten minutes is enough to state what should be done or changed in that plan. But if one speaks as though at an election campaign meeting, listing politically everything the Union Government has or has not done, naturally the time will not be enough.

She alleged a new form of repression in which the Centre prevented Chief Ministers opposed to it from speaking. Yet she herself spoke first. How then can it be claimed that only opponents were given less time while supporters received more? The Centre said the same rule applied to all. Union Minister Rajeev Shukla said the bell rang after ten minutes even for Congress Chief Ministers. Law Minister Ashwani Kumar said that with more than thirty Chief Ministers, ten minutes each already meant over five hours, and rejected the claim that the Centre had throttled the State's voice.

Nor is walking out new for our Chief Minister. She recently walked out of the Bengaluru meeting with the Karnataka Chief Minister on the Cauvery issue and returned to Chennai; she had similarly walked out of an earlier Cauvery River Authority meeting. The question is whether it is proper to walk out and then level sweeping accusations at the Union Government.

In her NDC speech she alleged that the Centre withheld a digitalisation licence from the Tamil Nadu Arasu Cable TV Corporation to favour a television service run by one family. But Information and Broadcasting Minister Manish Tewari had already explained that the decision would follow TRAI's recommendation that Central and State governments should not engage in cable-TV broadcasting, while adding that he had asked TRAI to reconsider its recommendation in relation to granting Tamil Nadu's corporation a digitalisation licence. Jayalalithaa also said AIADMK MPs had sought an appointment but were denied one; Tewari said AIADMK MPs had met him personally on the matter.

I am not saying everything the Union Government says must be accepted. As Chief Minister I attended many NDC meetings and voiced opposition. In 1969, at my first such meeting after becoming Chief Minister, with Finance Minister Mathiazhagan accompanying me, I called for examination of Centre-State relations. UNI noted that the Tamil Nadu Chief Minister alone had raised the issue there. At that meeting I also demanded nationalisation of banks. Union Finance Minister Morarji Desai strongly opposed me, but a few months later Prime Minister Indira Gandhi nationalised fourteen banks, and I invited her to Chennai and held a felicitation.

Again, at the NDC meeting of 21 March 1970, I declared that though those of us who had long raised a battle cry for Salem had come to power in Tamil Nadu, Salem itself had still not arrived. I demanded an announcement of the Salem Steel Plant, a fair share for Tamil Nadu in the additional ₹175 crore allocated to States, and no discrimination against Tamil Nadu in the ₹100 crore for employment in drought areas. Until those three demands were accepted, I said clearly on behalf of four crore Tamil people that I could not accept the Plan.

As a result, on 17 April 1970 Prime Minister Indira Gandhi announced in Parliament that the Salem Steel Plant would be started within the Fourth Plan. She later laid its foundation stone at a function in Salem under my chairmanship.

So my argument is not that one must support whatever the Centre says. Even when angry, preserving civility matters. In the 1971–72 Budget I said that if Tamil Nadu's appeals could be brushed aside even when there were those in the State who raised their voice for rights and those at the Centre who extended a hand for relations, what would happen if those in the State merely waited for favour and those at the Centre lacked generosity? I also said the Centre cannot be a deity dispensing boons while States remain devotees performing penance.

The Chief Minister's full speech has appeared in the newspapers. Had she read all of it there, it would have taken more than an hour. If every Chief Minister began saying, “We too will speak as long as Tamil Nadu's Chief Minister,” how long would the meeting have lasted? To claim, therefore, that Tamil Nadu itself was humiliated is an exaggeration, not a genuine charge. There was humiliation—the important question is: by whom?

With affection,
M.K.
29-12-2012
