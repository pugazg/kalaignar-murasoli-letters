**Udanpirappē,**

Union Home Minister P. Chidambaram contested the Sivaganga parliamentary constituency in 2009, won by a margin of 3,354 votes, served as Union Finance Minister and is now serving as Home Minister. The AIADMK candidate who opposed him in that election, Rajakannappan, filed a case saying that Chidambaram’s victory in that constituency was invalid.

As for Rajakannappan: he was first a minister in the AIADMK; later, when he was not given an opportunity there to contest an election, he joined the DMK, contested, became a DMK Member of the Legislative Assembly, and when he did not get a ministerial post here, rejoined the AIADMK and contested against P. Chidambaram in the last parliamentary election.

In the election case filed against Home Minister P. Chidambaram, Rajakannappan listed 29 allegations. That case is under hearing in the Madras High Court. Chidambaram, saying that the allegations in Rajakannappan’s petition were without basis, sought dismissal of the case. The Court has not yet examined whether those 29 allegations are true or whether there is evidence for them. In the meantime, on Chidambaram’s petition seeking dismissal, the judge said only that two of Rajakannappan’s 29 allegations had no evidence and would be struck out, while the remaining allegations would be tried. The judge said this only on 7 June 2012.

Not even twenty-four hours had passed after that order. Yet Chief Minister Jayalalithaa hurriedly issued a statement yesterday demanding that Chidambaram immediately resign from the Union Cabinet, and that if he did not resign the Prime Minister should remove him. What kind of Chief Minister have we got? A Tamil from our State is serving as Union Home Minister. Does this Ammaiyar suffer from such an excess of eagerness to make him lose office? Has any Court declared P. Chidambaram guilty? It has said only that the trial must now take place. Accepting part of Chidambaram’s plea, it struck two allegations and said the rest must be tried. Why such haste before that?

And what is the position of Chief Minister Jayalalithaa, who says that a person facing accusations must resign from office? How many judges have already ruled that you were guilty? What happened to the TANSI case? Did not the Supreme Court itself say that you should return to the Government the land you had purchased and act according to your conscience? What happened to the disproportionate-assets case? How many adjournments have you obtained so far? Has there been a ruling that you are innocent? Do not the people of Tamil Nadu themselves say that the very steps you have taken to drag out that case show that **you yourself are the guilty one**, and that all these measures are being taken only to conceal it? When that is your position, is there any justice in telling another person to resign his ministerial office?

This is not the only example. During the 2001 general election, court judgments had already found Jayalalithaa guilty to the point that she could not contest. Even so, she filed nomination papers in four constituencies, and they were rejected. At that time, the 6 May 2001 issue of *Ananda Vikatan* wrote in its editorial that Jayalalithaa’s approach to the election had crossed all bounds of justice and propriety; that even after her nominations had been rejected and she had lost eligibility to be elected to the Assembly, she had not altered her plan; that she went everywhere proclaiming that if the AIADMK alliance won she herself would be Chief Minister; that it was painful to see her willing to treat the people as fools on the assumption that they knew nothing of political conventions; and that if, after being barred from contesting, she had fully accepted the judgment and said, “Make our alliance win; if you do so, I shall place as Chief Minister on my behalf a leader acceptable to you and strive to provide good governance,” that would have been acceptable.

But do you know what Jayalalithaa did then? When the election results came and the AIADMK won a majority of seats, on the very day the results were still being announced she claimed that she had been chosen as leader of the legislature party, met the Governor and took the oath of office that same day. Tamil newspapers condemned it. That Jayalalithaa, who took office in such an irregular manner, later came under pressure to resign on 13 November 2001. All this is part of Tamil Nadu’s history.

It is this Jayalalithaa of such great fame(?) who now, when the Court has not even begun the trial, issues a statement telling the Union Home Minister to resign and asking the Prime Minister to remove him.

Like the saying “the lizard on the fence gives witness,” the BJP too, following Jayalalithaa’s statement, demanded that P. Chidambaram be removed from the Union Cabinet. Even *The Hindu*, reporting it, wrote that within a few hours of the High Court’s decision concerning Chidambaram, the BJP and AIADMK had called for his resignation. In an editorial today, 8 June 2012, *The Hindu* wrote:

> “The High Court’s decision allowing the trial against
> Mr. Chidambaram to take place should not be interpreted
> - as some of his political opponents have chosen to - as
> evidence of his guilt. No conclusion should be drawn about
> the merits of the petition and the calls for his resignation
> over this are unreasonable. The Court’s decision only
> means that the charges, with the exception of two that
> have been struck off, will now be examined in detail
> during the trial. However, in the interest of justice and in
> recognition of the centrality of elections to our constitutional
> framework, this should take place as expeditiously as
> possible”

The Tamil rendering printed immediately after that says:

> “The High Court’s order permitting the trial in the case against Mr. Chidambaram should not be regarded—as some of his political opponents have understood it—as evidence of guilt. No decision can now be reached about the merits of the case against him. Therefore there is no justice whatsoever in asking that he resign from office. Of the allegations mentioned in the case filed against him, the High Court has dismissed two allegations. The other allegations are to be examined in detail. Nevertheless, in order that justice may be established and with the object of recognising the impartial character of our Constitution, the trial must be conducted and completed as quickly as it ought to be.”

It states this categorically.

Like the expression “a pot of haste,” our Chief Minister Jayalalithaa and some others have issued statements demanding Chidambaram’s resignation. Responding, my dear friend P. Chidambaram himself said: “It is astonishing to think of the ignorance of some political leaders. There are 111 election cases pending against current MPs. In this case there are only allegations. The trial has not even begun. Not a single witness has been examined. We filed a petition to strike out the allegations. That petition has been accepted in part. If allegations are struck out, that is a setback to the person who filed the election case, not to me.” He has stated it unequivocally. After all, P. Chidambaram is not merely a politician; is he not also an able senior lawyer?

It is not just under legal principles to say that someone must resign the moment an accusation is made. The accusation must be established beyond doubt through detailed investigation and trial. It is only under Jayalalithaa’s rule that, on receiving a complaint, arrests are made in haste without first examining whether there is even prima facie evidence. If an example is needed, let Jayalalithaa ask the husband or relatives of the inseparable sister at her side; they will say that what I state is true. Election-related cases do not permit such unlawful half-baked action.

If, as Jayalalithaa and some others say, one must resign at the very first stage—before inquiry and judgment—then legal norms and Indian democracy themselves will become objects of mockery. **And this would apply to those who obtain countless “adjournments” too!**

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**9 June 2012**
