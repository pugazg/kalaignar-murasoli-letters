# 3733. Let Us Raise Our Voice!

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation carries Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English while preserving argument order, political directness, quotations, accusations, irony and rhetorical questions. The audited Tamil remains authoritative. `Udanpirappē` is retained as his distinctive political-familial address.

**Tamil source:** [Letter 3733](../../../chapters/3733-let-us-raise-our-voice.md)  
**Source pages:** [PDF 207](../../../pages/page-207.md)–[PDF 212](../../../pages/page-212.md)  
**Date:** 14 April 2013

**Udanpirappē,**

What *Dinamalar* and *Dina Thanthi* wrote a few months ago about the way the Tamil Nadu Legislative Assembly had functioned during its previous session has now come back to my mind.

*Dinamalar* had written, in substance, that DMDK MLAs had walked out many times and DMK MLAs had been expelled twice; that an Assembly includes the opposition too; that even if the ruling party possesses overwhelming numerical strength it must follow legislative democracy and ensure that opposition parties remain in the House; and that the AIADMK government was failing to do so. It said it was improper—and even a violation of rules—for the Chief Minister to make every announcement under Rule 110 merely so that members could speak only in praise, without debate or criticism. It contrasted this with Parliament, argued that democratic “murders” were being staged in the Tamil Nadu Assembly, and asked what justice there was in requiring everything to be accepted without debate. Such conduct, it said, was not good for democracy or the country.

A *Dina Thanthi* editorial observed that during the winter session of the Assembly, which had ended three days earlier, 23 bills had been passed in a single day—and without debate. Parties represented in the House ought to have sought debate on the bills and they should have been passed only after discussion. Just as a superfast express passing a station without stopping is called a “run-through”, it said, 23 bills had been passed as a “run-through” within a single hour without debate.

These criticisms appeared in newspapers generally supportive of the ruling party itself! Refusing even to hear the views of opposition parties—and, further, trying by every possible means to ensure that there is no opposition at all—is unbecoming of a good democratic government.

After the 2011 Assembly election, the DMDK became Tamil Nadu's principal opposition party. In that election it had been an ally of the AIADMK. What is that party going through under this government now? The DMDK and the DMK are not allies. Even so, it is painful to see the measures the AIADMK government takes against the opposition party DMDK. Government and opposition are two sides of the same coin. In a democracy each must show the other due respect.

Suddenly five DMDK MLAs meet the Chief Minister. They say they did so only to secure benefits for the people of their constituencies. If opposition MLAs wished to meet the Chief Minister on constituency matters, should they not have obtained permission from their party leadership? Was it not partly because that party worked alongside the AIADMK in the last election that the present rulers were able to come to power? Is this how gratitude is shown?

Did *Dinamalar* not report that a woman MLA from the Dalit community elected on the DMDK ticket cried out that she was “living in an environment without security”? Did it not report her as saying that when she attended government functions ruling-party members threatened to kill her, that there was no protection for life or property, and that women could not even go out safely?

When the Assembly met on 8 February 2013, an MLA elected on the DMDK ticket who was said to have broken away—Tamilazhagan—said openly in the House that because he had left the party, the government was doing good things for his constituency, and that other DMDK members too could benefit if they made the same decision. Was it proper to issue such an invitation in the Assembly itself, reducing legislative democracy to a mockery? DMDK MLAs protested. A dispute arose between the two sides. It was an internal matter among members elected on the DMDK ticket. Yet, on an allegation that a member who had broken party discipline was attacked, six members who remained firmly with the DMDK were proceeded against and suspended for six months.

Even before all this, on 3 February 2012, Vijayakanth, then Leader of the Opposition, was suspended from the Assembly for ten days on the ground that he had behaved in a way that obstructed proceedings. For those days he was also denied the benefits, salary, concessions and entitlements available to him as Leader of the Opposition and as an MLA. Nor did action stop in the Assembly. How many defamation cases have been filed against the Leader of the Opposition? And not against him alone—how many against newspapers, television channels and other media under this government? Even yesterday, a defamation case against CNN-IBN! What was their offence? A cartoon reportedly showed a journalist asking the Chief Minister how she ran the government, with the reply, “like a boss”. For that, a defamation case.

*Ananda Vikatan* published an article about Tamil Nadu ministers. I wrote that such an article had appeared. Immediately, every minister mentioned in that article separately filed a defamation case against me. What is happening in Tamil Nadu? Is this an Emergency? In the interests of Tamil Nadu's people, is no one allowed to criticise the government or the Chief Minister?

Last year, an MLA from the DMDK representing Tiruttani was arrested on some complaint. He obtained bail the very next day. The government immediately appealed to the High Court, got the bail cancelled, and for the past three days, we are told, four special teams have been trying to arrest him. What cruelty is this? Is this military rule? Dictatorship?

Even when the Viduthalai Chiruthaigal Katchi sought permission to conduct a procession and public meeting for Annal Ambedkar's birthday, this government refused. The party approached the High Court; its leader Thirumavalavan himself went to court and assured it that no untoward incident would occur, after which the High Court permitted the Ambedkar birthday procession. But do you know the painful part? The Tamil Nadu government is said to be appealing against that order. This anti-democratic approach is an injustice by the AIADMK government both to the Constitution of India and to Annal Ambedkar who shaped it. Likewise, permission for a TESO conference for Sri Lankan Tamils was refused by this government; only through the court's intervention could that conference be successfully held. Are these the practices of good government, or the cruelties of misrule?

When the DMK returned to office in 1989 after a thirteen-year interval, I began presenting the Budget. Under Jayalalithaa's leadership, AIADMK members tried to snatch the Budget from my hand, tear it up and attack me. They were suspended from House proceedings for a few days. When the Congress asked that even this suspension be withdrawn, I rose and said that neither I nor the government had the narrow-minded desire to conduct the House without the opposition; that what had happened should remain in the past and what happened henceforth should be good; and that the resolution passed in the heat and turmoil of that day should be withdrawn so that the Leader of the Opposition and the others could return and work in the House.

Only a few days ago I cited several examples of the magnanimity with which the DMK had acted in the Assembly. On 7 February 2011, when the Assembly met in the new legislature complex built by the DMK government at Omandurar, then Deputy Leader of the Opposition O. Panneerselvam moved a resolution under Assembly Rule 20(1), seeking leave of absence for Opposition Leader J. Jayalalithaa because doctors had advised her complete rest due to ill health. AIADMK whip K.A. Sengottaiyan seconded it, and it was passed by voice vote. Though the ruling DMK and its allies had a large majority, they accepted the opposition's resolution on humanitarian grounds without any objection. That was “political civility”.

Today, however, matters are conducted with the idea that there should be no opposition at all—or, if there is one, it should not be allowed to perform its duty. Some may attribute ulterior motives even to my expressing these views simply because I believe politics must retain a basic dignity, culture and civility. In a democracy, politics must be met politically; it is wrong to think success can be achieved through uncivilised conduct of this kind. I lovingly call upon everyone who believes in democratic values: come, let us unite and raise our voice!

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**14 April 2013**
