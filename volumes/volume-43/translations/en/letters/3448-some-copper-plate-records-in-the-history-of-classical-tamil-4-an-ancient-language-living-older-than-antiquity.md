# 3448. Some Copper-Plate Records in the History of Classical Tamil — 4: An Ancient Language Living Older Than Antiquity!

**Udanpirappē,**

After I assumed office as Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu for the fourth time, the DMK government sought to establish in proper form that Tamil is a classical language and place the demand before the Union Government. A draft report was prepared by Dr John Samuel, Director of the Institute of Asian Studies, and accepted by experts including Dr S. Agathiyalingam, Dr V. C. Kulandaisamy, Manavai Mustafa, Dr Avvai Natarajan and Dr Porko. The important portions of that report are as follows:

## Tamil, Shining as a High Independent Classical Language

### The Special Place of Classical Tamil in the Indian Context

Among the classical languages of the world, Tamil possesses an incomparable distinctiveness. It stands as a treasury of refined literature two thousand years old and as a protective repository of the older ornaments of Dravidian literature. With innumerable collections of poems, verse and songs, Tamil had already become established before the first year of the Christian era. Even after passing through many centuries, it continues to flourish with an unobstructed abundance and a splendid, vigorous growth, without losing its ever-youthful vitality.

In the family of Dravidian languages, Tamil shines in a high position, its course resembling a swelling river enriched by many connections and exchanges. Just as Sanskrit circulated in the northern regions of India, Tamil has remained a living language. In step with changes in Indian culture, it has maintained appropriate relationships with other languages while preserving its own distinctive character and undiminished strength.

Caldwell compared Tamil with root relationships in Celtic, Germanic, Latin and Greek and in the Iranian group within the Indo-European language family. Similarly, Thomas Burrow identified subtle connections between Finnish and the Dravidian languages. G. S. Balint studied relationships between Hungarian and Dravidian. Huber and Susumu Ono investigated Japanese and Korean in comparison with Tamil.

Tamil dances in the everyday life of six crore Tamils in Tamil Nadu in South India. It is also spoken and written by people living in Sri Lanka, Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand, Myanmar, Mauritius, Trinidad and the Fiji Islands. The governments of Sri Lanka and Singapore, the report states, have also declared Tamil an official language.

Bearing artistic, literary and cultural wealth comparable in the report’s framing to Egyptian, Sumerian and Indus Valley civilisation, Tamil enables researchers to reckon the antiquity of the proto-Dravidian tradition and the development of ancient Tamil across thousands of years. Even amid so many connections, the report argues that Tamil alone remains a language older than antiquity and still living today.

The classification that defines the Tamil language, the principles embedded in its literary creations and its grammatical rules in detail began more than two thousand years ago. *Tolkappiyam* thus supplied a foundation for the refined formation of literary verse. Its rules and norms, phonological and linguistic practices, and rules for handling foreign words are clearly set out, providing room for useful development of the language. The scholars of this land, the report says, were already nurturing two thousand years ago the idea by which this ancient and highly developed language can now be described as classical.

Gilbert Slater is cited as saying that Tamil attained classical-language qualification even before Tamil speakers entered into contact with Greek. Dr Winslow is cited as describing Tamil as richer than Greek, superior to Latin, and more powerful than English and German. According to the French Indologist Pierre Meile, the Sangam songs of Classical Tamil stand as a challenge to the refined poetic collections of Greek.

### The Excellence of Tamil Realised Late

Walter Elliot, Ellis, Gover, Winslow, Caldwell, Pope and Vinson are cited as having observed, even in their own periods, that study of Dravidian Tamil culture was generally neglected. Dr G. U. Pope is said to have written as early as 1910 about the British government’s neglect of Tamil literature. Though Tamil was an ancient, rich and cultivated language inferior to none in distinction, the report says that most people thought of it merely as a language spoken by people living in a region far from the centre of the British Empire. Neither the Indian government nor the universities, it says, had fully recognised the stature of Tamil literature; those who researched South Indian languages therefore resembled rare workers searching the deep sea for pearls.

In the United States, Tamil began to be studied both as a language of the geographically vast Indian country and as a language associated with a Dravidian people and social concerns. Tamil obtained an important place in language-training programmes at the Universities of Pennsylvania, Chicago and Kansas and in summer schools.

International scholars and Tamil researchers, the report says, came to recognise that Tamil studies were indispensable to a complete understanding of Indian cultural elements. Study of Sanskrit alone was not sufficient; any study of Indian culture could be complete only when the rich artistic, cultural and literary components of Tamil, the oldest of the Dravidian languages, were taken into account. The report cites the noted scholar of Indian languages Dr Suniti Kumar Chatterji — recalling that his interest in Tamil led him to adopt the Tamil name “Nanneri Murugan” — as saying that in present Indian culture and religious philosophy the Dravidian language forms the warp and the Aryan language the weft, and that the Dravidian contribution to the wealth of Indian culture is no less than fifty per cent.

Sangam Tamil literature is described as a refined and modern Indian literature: as ancient as earlier Sanskrit works yet continuing to live alongside modern creations. A. K. Ramanujan, in a work identified in the source as *Ilakkiya Iyarkaiyadaivu*, is cited as saying that Tamil, one of India’s two classical languages, alone is also a contemporary living Indian language, continuing from ancient antiquity as an accepted spoken language among the people.

Dr Kamil Zvelebil is cited as giving three reasons for regarding the celebrated Sangam poems as classical. First, Tamil scholars, historians and critics praise them as classical, just as parts of national literatures elsewhere are celebrated. Second, they form a fully “completed” literary corpus and yet continue in a living oral tradition; their valued words, not open to alteration with changing times, remain in a fixed poetic form and are part of a classical tradition. Third, they possess a complete framework of language-based prosody and conventions rooted in experience. They constitute the literary continuum and crowning achievement of a complete, incomparable culture. The report cites Zvelebil’s *The Smile of Murugan*, page 50.

Because Tamil holds the leading place among the Dravidian language family, it has stood through many centuries as enduring southern Tamil, gaining growth and eminence, embracing influences and mingling with others. Like other Dravidian languages, it shows comparative relationships with features of Indo-European and eastern languages. Of more than twenty-five languages in the Dravidian family, the report describes Tamil as the foremost.

Roots of Tamil words, it states, can be found in all the classical languages of the world. Tamil has produced as masterpieces both heroic *puram* poetry and elevated love-filled *akam* poetry, as well as ethical works. These literatures speak of a universal humanism not confined to religion. Tamil also possesses a wealth of devotional religious poetry. It has left an enduring Tamil imprint on all the languages now present in the country.

Since the discovery of the Indus Valley civilisation following excavations at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro over the previous fifty years or more, the report says, the substantial contribution of Tamil- or Dravidian-speaking peoples to Indian culture as a whole had come to be recognised more widely. Professor R. D. Banerji, identified as the discoverer of Mohenjo-daro, is cited as having established that the Indus Valley civilisation existed many centuries before the arrival of the Aryans and was a remarkable pre-Aryan civilisation; its people were later identified as Dravidians or ancestors of the Dravidian people, related to the various communities of South India who speak Dravidian languages.

The report states beyond doubt that Tamils had already developed their literary and grammatical theories with great skill two thousand years ago and that these were in no respect inferior to those of any classical language in the world. Although Tamil took some words from Sanskrit, it retained its distinctiveness and structural order by treating them as Tamil words or Tamilising them. Dr Burrow is cited as having listed many subtleties of word formation that Tamil contributed to the languages of North India.

Although Tamil made a very large contribution to Indian culture, the report says, recognition appropriate to its stature was delayed. At Dakar University in Senegal, Tamil, Arabic, Persian and Sanskrit were being taught as classical languages in the department of classical studies; yet in the universities here Tamil had long before been removed from such a list, leaving Arabic, Persian and Sanskrit alone accepted as classical languages.

A century earlier, Tamil scholar V. Ko. Suryanarayana Sastri had described Tamil as a high independent classical language and wanted it crowned with classical-language status. That plea stirred many scholars.

Every language community with a literary heritage identifies a particular period in its social and literary setting as its classical age. According to some scholars, the period from 500 to 300 BCE was the classical age of the Greeks. The period from 500 BCE to 1110 CE is described as the classical period of Sanskrit.

The period from the Sangam age through the sixth century CE, the report says, may be described as Tamil’s classical age, because incomparable literature and elevated linguistic thought flowered in it. Those contributions created the antiquity and rich excellence of the Dravidian tradition. Many noted researchers, it says, insist that any study of Indian cultural heritage that fails to count the great cultural achievements of the Dravidians will remain incomplete and deficient.

International scholars now accept Tamil as one of the classical languages of the world. Universities in Tamil Nadu have come forward to declare ancient Tamil a classical language alongside Sanskrit, Arabic and Persian. Sanskrit scholar C. R. Sankaran, after studying the section on phonology in the two-thousand-year-old *Tolkappiyam*, is cited as concluding that the Tamil grammatical tradition’s analysis of the internal nature of sound, its effort to establish sounds on the basis of form and classification, and its place in determining meaning are not found even in Panini’s *Ashtadhyayi*. The ancient Tamil grammatical tradition may therefore be counted as a highly significant contribution to the history of languages.

### Tamil as a Classical Language

Among the languages of India, Tamil stands with a distinctive vigour. It contains rich theories of inner and outer literature, prosody, poetics and grammatical limits. Though Tamil and Sanskrit existed contemporaneously as two languages, Tamil, the report says, remained independent and incomparable without losing its vitality or structural integrity under Sanskrit influence.

### Ancient Tamil Flowering as a Classical Language

Although Tamil possesses the recognised qualities of universal literary heritage, antiquity, richness and simplicity, it had until then been treated as a regional language and classified among the modern Indian languages. Apart from Sanskrit, the report says, no other Indian language has an ancient tradition like Tamil.

The Constitution of India defines the preservation and protection of the high heritage of our composite culture as a fundamental duty of the Indian citizen. In that setting, the report concludes, it is our duty to accept Tamil as a classical language.

This report, sent by the DMK government to the Union Ministry of Education with an appropriate historical background and evidence requesting that Tamil be declared a classical language, was forwarded to the Central Institute of Indian Languages at Mysore for examination and comment.
