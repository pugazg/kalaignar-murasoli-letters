# 3647. What Becomes of Neutrality, Justice and Conscience?

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.”

**Date:** 19 August 2012

**Udanpirappē,**

If there is a case that has become an unprecedented wonder in Indian legal history by continuing for more than fifteen years, it is the ₹66-crore disproportionate-assets case against our Chief Minister Jayalalithaa. The case, begun in 1996, was transferred by the Supreme Court in 2003 to a Special Court in Bengaluru; even after that, eight more years rolled by. Two judges had already heard the matter in the Special Court, and the third judge then hearing it, Mallikarjunaiah, was due to retire that month.

In this case, days had been consumed by explanation after explanation, question after question, adjournment after adjournment. Each time a plea was made in the Special Court and an adverse order was passed, an appeal was taken to the Karnataka High Court; if the result there too was adverse, the same issue was carried again to the Supreme Court, consuming a few more months. Then some new ground was raised in the Special Court to delay the case again. This had gone on year after year, leaving both the Special Court judge and the counsel appearing for the Karnataka Government exhausted and under severe mental strain.

There is a saying about something that first bites a goat, then a cow and finally a human being. In much the same way, the Jayalalithaa side had now gone so far as to argue that the very judge hearing the case was not qualified. The Karnataka High Court was hearing the question of whether that judge was qualified. In the midst of all this, senior advocate B.V. Acharya, who had argued the case for the State from the beginning, had recently resigned as Special Public Prosecutor.

There was an irony even in that. In his resignation letter, Acharya described with pain the pressure and threats he had faced. He said that by resigning he had allowed the interested parties to achieve their objective; that throughout the trial he had been subjected to hardships he could scarcely describe; that their sole aim had been to remove him as Special Public Prosecutor; that when inducements and threats failed, proceedings were initiated against him; and that even after courts dismissed them, petitions were made against him to the Chief Justice and the Governor. The repeated attacks, he said, had caused him great mental anguish, and he had been left helpless.

A case was filed in the Karnataka High Court alleging that Acharya had misused public office and committed financial irregularities in connection with the BMS Educational Trust in Bengaluru. The court dismissed the case for want of any basis and imposed a fine of ₹50,000 on the complainant.

The source then reproduces a report from *The Hindu* describing Acharya's resignation and his allegation that interested parties had subjected him to “untold hardship and embarassment”, used inducements and threats, filed proceedings with an oblique motive and eventually brought a private complaint in the Lokayukta Court. The report also notes that the High Court quashed that complaint, imposed a ₹50,000 fine, and observed that there was “sufficient weight” in Acharya's contention that the complaint had been motivated by an attempt to humiliate and coerce him into leaving the post.

Two days before I wrote this letter, when the disproportionate-assets case against Jayalalithaa and Sasikala came up in the Karnataka High Court, Acharya himself appeared and sought to be relieved from the case. Tamil newspapers carried only that part of the news. But *The Hindu* reported under a headline saying that Acharya need not resign. Justice Mohan Shantana Goudar asked Acharya to assist the High Court on the questions raised in the petitions, because he had already argued the issue concerning appointment of the Special Court judge. When Acharya said he had resigned and had been asked to continue until alternative arrangements were made, the judge said there was no need for him to resign and asked him to assist the court. Most newspapers, however, seem to have concealed this part of the story—perhaps out of fear of Jayalalithaa's side. Why?

That is not all. The extent to which the Jayalalithaa side had made a mockery of India's judicial process was evident from Acharya's special interview to *Junior Vikatan*. He said that when he was appointed Special Public Prosecutor in 2005 under Supreme Court directions, he believed the case would finish within six months. Instead, over seven years, the accused side filed petition after petition in the Special Court, High Court and Supreme Court, and repeatedly went on appeal. Even then, two petitions filed by Sasikala were pending in the Supreme Court. If the disproportionate-assets case had hardly moved from where it began, what could he do?

For about a year, he said, various conspiratorial attempts had been made to force him out of the case: defamatory petitions to the Governor and High Court judge, pamphlets in the courts, posters, complaints in the media, and then an objection that he should not simultaneously hold the offices of Advocate General and Special Public Prosecutor. Those behind the attempt apparently expected him to resign as Special Public Prosecutor, but he instead resigned as Advocate General. Finally, people were instigated to file defamatory cases against him before the Karnataka Lokayukta Court and the High Court. One case was dismissed at the outset for want of basic evidence. In another case alleging fraud in an educational institution, the High Court rebuked the complainant for defaming an honest man and imposed a ₹50,000 fine.

Acharya said the unnecessary pressure had caused him intense mental torture. At his age, he asked, did he need that kind of mental torture? He had no desire to continue appearing in the case and facing such pressure. He was helpless. He had even written to the Karnataka Home Secretary that his resignation would be welcome news to Jayalalithaa's side.

Thus this senior advocate openly told the world what he had endured. However much attempts were made to prolong the case, the public and the world had by then understood how substantial the documentary evidence underlying this disproportionate-assets case was. Why, then, keep dragging it out? As the saying goes, one fears the road only when there is weight hidden in one's lap!

Here is another example. M. Mariappan had filed an appeal in August 1998 before the Coimbatore Principal Subordinate Judge against an order of the Special Deputy Collector (Stamps), Coimbatore. Fourteen years later, with that appeal still pending, he approached the Madras High Court asking for an expeditious disposal. The High Court observed that keeping an appeal pending for fourteen years was disturbing and certainly not palatable, and that courts should adopt a pragmatic, practical, purposeful and common-sense approach so that a litigant's cause was resolved early. It recalled the saying that a litigant is mortal while litigation is immortal, and directed the Coimbatore court to decide the appeal within three months in the interests of neutrality, justice, good conscience and prudence and to prevent a failure of justice.

If that judgment of the Madras High Court applied to a case that had lasted fourteen years, did it apply—or did it not apply—to the disproportionate-assets case involving Jayalalithaa's side that had been going on for sixteen years?

Those who assumed office contrary to the Constitution; those who tried to settle scores by planting a ganja case even on the relative of a High Court judge; those who bent the law and used the police to arrest and imprison people they disliked; those who misused every law available to them, including the Goondas Act, against former ministers and DMK functionaries in the name of land grabbing—it was they who were dragging out this disproportionate-assets case in this manner.

People of this country! Those who claim neutrality! Good people! You yourselves say whether this is right.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**19-8-2012**
