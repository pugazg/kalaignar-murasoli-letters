# 3462. Let It Serve Our Journey!

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This letter draws lessons in steadfastness from source-framed accounts of Socrates, Prophet Muhammad, Jesus and Gandhi, and closes by applying those examples to the movement’s own journey. The historical and religious anecdotes are translated as the author presents them; they are not externally reconciled or expanded.

**Tamil source:** [Letter 3462](../../../chapters/3462-nam-payanathirkup-payanpada.md)  
**Source pages:** [PDF 271](../../../pages/page-271.md)–[PDF 273](../../../pages/page-273.md)  
**Date:** 28 February 2010

**Udanpirappē,**

Even knowing that they were going to hand him the cup of poison and take away his life, Socrates, the wise old man of Greece, did not abandon the principle he held or retreat from his ideal. He remained stubborn to the end that he would say what had to be said to the world. After taking leave of his wife Xanthippe, even at the moment when he was about to embrace death, he called his friend Crito close and asked him to return to the neighbour the chicken that had been borrowed on credit, thereby teaching probity and honesty. He drank the poison imposed by the court, gave up his life and shines as a beacon in the history of humankind.

Yesterday we joyfully offered greetings and salutations to the hero of history, the great Prophet Muhammad, who lives forever in every home and every heart of Islamic people. Could he have felt satisfied that people immediately accepted his ideas? No, no. The whole world knows how patiently he had to swim against the current to imprint the ideas he held upon people’s minds.

Here is an incident offered as evidence that his patience bore fitting fruit.

The Prophet used to pass through certain streets every day for his walk and bath. Whenever he went that way, a woman who was consumed by jealousy at his fame would, from the upper floor, empty a basket of rubbish onto his head. He would brush off the rubbish that had been thrown on him, immerse himself in the pond and wash his body.

Because that woman had not accepted Islam and bore boundless hatred toward the Prophet, this festival of throwing rubbish went on every day.

One day, when the Prophet walked near that house on the same street, the rubbish he expected was not thrown on his head. Filled with surprise, the noble Prophet is said to have asked the neighbours what had happened to that lady and why she had not thrown rubbish. On hearing that the woman was unwell, Prophet Muhammad is said to have hurried upstairs to her house, enquired after her health, and helped her recover quickly.

The mind of the woman who had hated the Prophet and thrown rubbish at him every day is said to have changed. Astonished by and admiring the magnanimity of Islam and the Prophet who practised it, she regretted her action and joined Islam — such an incident is told.

It is because that man of character endured opposition, ridicule, mockery and jeering — even such clownish acts as throwing rubbish — that today his faith flies its flag high to the praise of the world.

Even when those seated upon thrones raged, “Carry the cross on your shoulder,” that treasure of thought carried it exactly so.

Even when the order came, “You will be nailed to the very cross you carried,” and nails were driven into his hands, feet, neck and forehead and blood flowed like a pool, was it not that noble man who, without losing resolve and with a single ideal, did not hesitate to give up his life and is today honoured throughout the world as the foremost figure of Christianity — as grace and as love? Even knowing that this punishment would be given to him, and without wishing punishment upon others, was it not Jesus who stood at the summit of the principle he held, like the pinnacle of a tower and the finial upon that pinnacle?

Vowing, “I shall abolish religious divisions and unite my people; I shall see a righteous order without caste or religious difference,” he walked to Navakali and gave his life — a life honoured by the world — as prey to a wretch’s religious hatred and to the three bullets he fired. What was he then? Was he someone who abandoned his post as a lawyer in South Africa and came to rule the Indian nation? No. He laboured so that this country might be freed from the chains of slavery. Were not those three bullets the reward he received?

If such rewards are what ideals and firmness of principle receive, those who can bear them — those who are ready to receive such rewards — disregard opposition, thinking that this good world may go on yearning in remembrance of them.

Udanpirappē, do you know why I remind you of these heart-melting events? These incidents will stand as strong supports for our journey. Therefore, let us step forward without fear; let us perform our duty without letting our eyes sleep!

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**28-2-2010**

## Letter-specific notes

1. The Socrates, Prophet Muhammad, Jesus and Gandhi narratives are preserved as source-framed examples of steadfastness; no outside historical or theological reconciliation is inserted.
2. The source’s repetitions, rhetorical questions and concluding movement exhortation are retained rather than compressed.
3. The place-name form corresponding to `நவகாளி` is rendered as **Navakali**, following the source form rather than silently normalising it from outside knowledge.
4. No canonical Tamil defect was suspected during source-check.
