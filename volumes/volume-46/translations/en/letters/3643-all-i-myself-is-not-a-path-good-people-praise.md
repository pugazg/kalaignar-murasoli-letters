# 3643. ‘Everything Is Me’ Is Not a Path Good People Praise!

## English translation

**Udanpirappē,**

Chief Minister Jayalalithaa’s announcement of 23-7-2012 says that the five Anna Universities of Technology are to be merged with Anna University, Chennai, and made into a single university! Jayalalithaa, acting with the sole intention of reversing every progressive decision taken by the DMK Government, is now intervening even in students’ education in a way that will affect Tamil Nadu’s future generations. That is deeply painful.

As far as the Anna Universities of Technology are concerned, the very name “Anna” seems to remind Amma Jayalalithaa only of the DMK! She forgets—or, though remembering it, keeps it in her heart like fire—that M.G.R. himself founded her party in Anna’s name as the “Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam,” and that Anna’s portrait appears on their party flag. If, to mark Anna’s centenary, a library of a scale said to be the largest in Asia is built, the thought that arises in Jayalalithaa’s mind is not that it is a library bearing Anna’s name, but that Karunanidhi built it. Immediately she sets about trying to shift that library.

In the same manner, plans are under way to make the entrance of M.G.R.’s memorial taller than that of Anna’s memorial.

It is in this sequence that, when five Universities of Technology bearing Anna’s name were established during the DMK Government, she does not think of how greatly students in all parts of Tamil Nadu would benefit. Instead, she acts as though saying: “What do I care what happens to students? See, I have removed Anna’s name from five places.” This is just how, almost as soon as she came to power last year, she acted in a manner that would ruin school education by declaring that the Samacheer Kalvi scheme could not be accepted and that all those textbooks should be thrown away. Fortunately, because the court acted with great care, the Samacheer Kalvi scheme survived. Now, in the same line, she says she will merge into one the five Anna Universities of Technology that were created to strengthen engineering higher education.

If Kamaraj laid the foundation for students’ elementary education, it can be said that the government that firmly laid the foundation for higher education for Tamil Nadu’s students was the DMK Government. During DMK rule, universities, medical colleges, engineering colleges and arts colleges spread and multiplied across the State. Every effort needed to enable students to obtain higher education was systematically undertaken. Indeed, recognising the importance of higher education, it was during DMK rule that a separate ministry was created for it. If someone brings even that to our Chief Minister’s attention, it would not be surprising if she announced that, because it was created during DMK rule, that too is unnecessary and one minister for the whole Education Department is enough again.

Writing on this merger, the English daily *Deccan Chronicle* of 23-7-2012 reported that senior educationists, though they had welcomed it initially, now said that in the long run the decision was not good: **“It was not a good move in the long run, they pointed out”.** It further published the educationists’ view that decentralisation was beneficial in all fields including education; that it would improve quality and make monitoring and regulation easier; and that centralisation, by contrast, would create many problems: **“The Professors argued that decentralization was always good, including in education, which would enhance the quality of education and help in easy monitoring and regulation, while centralisation would only open a Pandora’s Box.”**

The same English paper wrote that the principal of a renowned engineering college said that merging Anna University in this fashion would not help engineering education in any way, and that printing and issuing certificates, conducting examinations and supervising more than 500 engineering colleges would create enormous difficulties.

The *Hindu* dated 25-7-2012 published a large report under the heading **“Anna University merger worries part-time students”**, with the line **“Students say their course will not be recognized by Anna University, Chennai örgjiThis will make it difficult for them to join a Ph.D. Programme in AU, Chennai”**.

What does it matter who writes? What does it matter who says it? Is there a government in Tamil Nadu that will listen to all this and think? Is this not a government of barren obstinacy that insists the hare it has caught has only three legs!

Speaking for the AIADMK in the Assembly debate on the Higher Education Department grant on 3-5-2007, today’s Minister C.V. Shanmugam did not say that establishing Anna Universities at Tiruchi and Coimbatore was wrong. Nor did he say universities should not be divided. What he said was: “Instead of saying Tiruchi, Madurai, Coimbatore Anna University, I wish to suggest that it would be better to say Anna University, Tiruchi; Anna University, Coimbatore—just as one says IIM Bangalore, IIM Delhi, Allahabad.”

The DMK did not suddenly announce its decision to establish Anna University in five regions of Tamil Nadu. Even in the election manifesto issued before the 2006 general election, on page 39, we had said: “We will find a way to remove the practical administrative confusion caused by affiliating all engineering colleges to Anna University and to ensure proper, uniform education at every level.” We did not merely say this and remain idle. In keeping with “We will do what we say; we will say what we do,” after winning the 2006 election and assuming office, we consulted educationists in Tamil Nadu. In the 2006–07 Budget itself, page 20, paragraph 50, we announced: “In keeping with the election manifesto, because the demand for engineering and technical education is increasing and the need for specialised higher degree programmes in that education is growing, this Government has decided to establish Universities of Technology like Anna University, Chennai—one at Tiruchi and one at Coimbatore.”

Our work for higher education did not begin only then. Even when I was Chief Minister from 1969 to 1976, permission was given for 68 arts colleges to be started in Tamil Nadu on a scale not seen earlier: 18 government colleges and 50 private colleges; among the government colleges, 8 women’s colleges; among the private colleges, 21 women’s colleges.

When India attained Independence on 15 August 1947, there were only 20 universities in the whole country. Tamil Nadu had only two. In 1971 India had 83 universities; Tamil Nadu had three. The third university started in Tamil Nadu was the Tamil Nadu Agricultural University at Coimbatore on 1 June 1971. Even that Agricultural University arose from an experience in November 1969, when, accepting Punjab Chief Minister Gurnam Singh’s invitation to attend Guru Nanak’s 500th birth anniversary celebrations, I visited the agricultural university at Ludhiana and participated in a function there. It was as a result of seeing that university that the Agricultural University was started at Coimbatore.

Not only for agriculture: the first university in Asia exclusively for veterinary research was started in Chennai during DMK rule, when this humble man was Chief Minister.

Likewise, a separate university for legal education in the name of Annal Ambedkar was inaugurated during DMK rule by inviting then President K.R. Narayanan, when I was Chief Minister.

Bharathidasan University at Tiruchi, Manonmaniam Sundaranar University at Tirunelveli, Thanthai Periyar University at Salem, Thiruvalluvar University at Vellore and the Tamil Nadu M.G.R. Medical University at Chennai were all started during DMK rule. The Tamil Nadu Teachers Education University too has been started in Chennai.

As a result of the DMK Government’s sustained efforts pointing out that Tamil Nadu did not have even one Central University, the Central University at Tiruvarur was established. The Union Government approved the establishment of the Indian Institute of Management at Tiruchi, and the DMK Government provided the land required for it.

Are there really so many universities in Tamil Nadu? The United States, with a population of 27.6 crore, has 2,500 universities; Japan, with 12.7 crore, has 726; Germany, with 8.2 crore, has 350; England, with a smaller population than Tamil Nadu at 6 crore, has 125. Yet India, with 110 crore people, has only 415 universities. Of those, Tamil Nadu, with 7 crore people, has only 53 universities in total, including 28 deemed universities. In such a situation, Tamil Nadu Chief Minister Jayalalithaa is engaged in the great achievement of conducting a closing ceremony for five universities! Everywhere else people strive to create new educational institutions and universities. Can one see anywhere a Chief Minister striving to close five universities already functioning? If others are engaged in creation, is she alone trying to prove that she possesses the ability (?) to engage in destruction? Her sole concern seems to be: “Was this something Karunanidhi did? First conduct a closing ceremony for it,” rather than the welfare of the people or students.

In 2009 a committee headed by Dr Yash Pal was constituted for the renewal of higher education. Its report said that the affiliated-college system places a heavy burden on universities in administering curricula, examinations and college standards; that capable colleges suffer because of delay, control and insufficient support from university authorities; and, at the same time, capable universities are affected by the narrow outlook of colleges and their negative role in university functioning.

Infrastructure required for research exists only in a few colleges, and even there only a few teachers engage in research; student participation in research has largely been neglected. Universities, however, possess research infrastructure, and most teachers there remain continuously engaged in research. Necessary financial support can be obtained from Central agencies and foreign institutions. Thus universities become centres of capable people, making it possible to plan afresh and introduce new curricula. If universities are kept at a scale conducive to growth, rather than becoming too large to administer efficiently under the burden of affiliated colleges, new curricula can be implemented quickly and properly.

To study higher education in India and recommend what was needed for its growth, the Union Government appointed the Dr Radhakrishnan Committee in 1948, the Dr Kothari Education Commission in 1966, the Dr Sam Pitroda Committee in 2006 and the Dr Yash Pal Committee in 2009. All these bodies stressed that new universities should be started and their number increased for the growth of higher education in India. The Dr Sam Pitroda and Dr Yash Pal Committees recommended that 1,500 universities be established in India within the next ten years. Yet here is a revolutionary (!) idea that never occurred to any of these scholars—but has occurred to our Chief Minister!

Until 1978, all engineering colleges in Tamil Nadu were affiliated to the Universities of Madras and Madurai. In 1978, during AIADMK rule, only the engineering college at Guindy, Chennai, was transformed into Anna University. Until 2001 all other engineering colleges were affiliated to six arts and science universities in Tamil Nadu. In 2001, the 247 engineering colleges then in Tamil Nadu were affiliated to Anna University, making it an affiliating university. It was realised that administering 247 engineering colleges spread throughout the State under one university was unworkable. That is why, from 1-2-2007, an Anna University of Technology at Coimbatore with 200 affiliated engineering colleges and one at Tiruchi with 85 colleges began functioning. In 2007 one was started at Tirunelveli with 67 colleges, and in 2010 universities were started at Chennai with 170 colleges and at Madurai with 49 colleges.

Thus, so that all five Anna Universities of Technology in Tamil Nadu could focus primarily on research, Anna University at Guindy, Chennai, functioned as a unitary university.

But after coming to power last year, the AIADMK Government unilaterally announced—without consulting educationists and without considering student welfare or excellence in research—that the Anna Universities of Technology would be abolished and all 571 engineering colleges in Tamil Nadu would be affiliated to Anna University, Guindy; it also enacted the necessary law.

At a time when countries around the world are increasing the number of universities year after year, Tamil Nadu Chief Minister Jayalalithaa has offered a new idea as her own discovery: “Merely starting a large number of universities will not in itself produce any improvement in the quality of education.”

If Jayalalithaa’s view is acceptable, should she not announce that all colleges in Tamil Nadu will come under the University of Madras alone? Is she going to make such an announcement in future? Will she say there is no need for District Collectors in the various districts of Tamil Nadu and that everything will operate under the direct supervision of the Chief Secretary? Will she abolish the offices of the district Superintendents of Police and announce that they will all function centrally under the direct leadership of the DGP? Is she going to reverse everything that has been decentralised and bring it under one umbrella through centralisation?

When more than one University of Technology functions, healthy competition among them for development will prevail. Since universities work for the country’s economic and higher-education advancement, the regions where they are located can develop well. In the districts and surrounding regions where universities are situated, research and social-development projects appropriate to their nature and standards can be implemented more extensively and rapidly.

If there is only one university in Chennai, students from distant towns will be compelled to travel to Chennai for every task—especially enquiries concerning marks and work related to research theses. This will cause needless travel, mental strain, expenditure of money and loss of time for parents and students alike.

Central Government bodies such as the University Grants Commission and the Department of Science and Technology, as well as similar institutions in the United States and Europe, provide grants to Indian universities for research and higher education. There is an opportunity for such grants to come to Tamil Nadu in greater measure. For example, if a grant of ₹5 crore is given to one university, six universities together could receive ₹30 crore. Through this, universities can attain proper growth without obstruction or hindrance.

A study by the National Knowledge Commission and other Indian technical bodies found that India still needs 1,500 universities and that they should be established quickly. Therefore, the number of universities currently in Tamil Nadu should be increased, not reduced.

If five Universities of Technology are merged into one, a situation may arise in which many of Tamil Nadu’s science and arts universities too are called upon to merge into one.

Because universities are located in different parts of the State, industrial growth can take place separately in those regions. More students can also remain with their parents while pursuing higher education. This gives them greater safety, a healthy sense of well-being, accommodation and peace of mind, which in turn supports their intellectual growth, research and other advancement. Industrial growth in the respective districts and regions will also reduce population congestion in Chennai.

The University Grants Commission has said that roughly up to 100 colleges may be affiliated to a single university. But under Jayalalithaa’s present decision, 571 colleges are affiliated to Anna University. This will increase Anna University’s administrative burden. Its teachers will be unable to concentrate on research. Students from the southern districts will be severely affected.

Is it a matter of pride if we say, “During DMK rule we created five new universities, and during AIADMK rule we created five more”? Or is it a matter of pride to abolish the five universities created during DMK rule? Is reversing a progressive measure and making it retrogressive what is called development? Are people thinking, “Why did we vote for this AIADMK with its negative outlook?” That is enough for now!

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**26-07-2012**

கழக ஆட்சியில் ஐந்து பல்கலைக் கழகங்களை புதிதாகக் கொண்டு வந்தோம்; அ.தி.மு.க. ஆட்சியில் மேலும் ஐந்து பல்கலைக் கழகங்களை உருவாக்கினோம் என்றால் பெருமையா? தி.மு. கழக ஆட்சியில் உருவாக்கிய ஐந்து பல்கலைக் கழகங்களை இல்லாமல் செய்து பெருமையா? முற்போக்கு நடவடிக்கையை மாற்றி, பிற்போக்காக்குவது தான் வளர்ச்சியா? ஏன்தான் எதிர்மறை எண்ணம் கொண்ட இந்த அ.தி.மு.க.விற்கு வாக்களித்தோமோ என்று எண்ணுகிறார்களா? அது போதும் இப்போது!
