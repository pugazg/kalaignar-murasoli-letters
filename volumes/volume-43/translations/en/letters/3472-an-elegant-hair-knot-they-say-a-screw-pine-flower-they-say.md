# 3472. An Elegant Hair-Knot, They Say; a Screw-Pine Flower, They Say...

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This is the first of three consecutive letters under the same proverbial image. The actual letter-start title at PDF 326 uses short `ஒய்யாரக்...`, while the printed contents independently uses long `ஓய்யாரக்...`; both source layers are preserved. The letter answers Jayalalithaa’s demand that Union Minister A. Raja resign on moral grounds by recounting, from the author’s perspective, the 2001 nomination, disqualification, chief-ministerial appointment and court chronology concerning Jayalalithaa. Legal and political assertions are retained as source-attributed claims.

**Tamil source:** [Letter 3472](../../../chapters/3472-oyyaarak-kondaiyaam-thaazhampoovaam.md)  
**Source pages:** [PDF 326](../../../pages/page-326.md)–[PDF 331](../../../pages/page-331.md)  
**Date:** 30 May 2010

**Udanpirappē,**

Jayalalithaa is holding a demonstration in Chennai today, saying that there is an accusation against Union Minister Thambi Raja and asking why he has not accepted moral responsibility and resigned from his ministerial post. But when corruption complaints were made against this lady in the past and judges in courts made observations and charges concerning her, did she accept moral responsibility and resign from her post?

Though she has claimed in her statement that she was acquitted in twelve cases, the people of Tamil Nadu know very well how those acquittals came about and what judgments judges had delivered earlier in those very cases. Some may have forgotten them; it is only to remind them that I write this letter.

In the 2001 general election, Jayalalithaa did not contest in even a single constituency. Filing of nominations began on 16 April. That very day, she filed her nomination for Krishnagiri constituency before Returning Officer M. Madhivanan. On 18 April, she filed her nomination for Andipatti constituency before Returning Officer Jaya. On 23 April, the last day for filing nominations, nominations were also filed on Jayalalithaa’s behalf in Bhuvanagiri and Pudukkottai constituencies.

Scrutiny of nominations took place on the 24th. Krishnagiri Returning Officer Madhivanan, after examining the nomination, announced that Jayalalithaa’s nomination was rejected because she had been sentenced to more than two years’ imprisonment and because she had filed nominations in more than two constituencies. In the TANSI land-deal case, Jayalalithaa had been sentenced to three years’ imprisonment.

Section 8 of the Representation of the People Act says that a person sentenced to imprisonment for more than two years cannot contest an election. (Under Section 8(3) of the Representation of the People Act, 1951, a person convicted by a court and sentenced to imprisonment for not less than two years becomes disqualified from contesting an election from the date of conviction. That disqualification continues for a further six years from the date of release from prison. This is the Election Commission’s rule.) The Election Commission of India issued this circular in 1997.

Siddhartha Shankar Ray, former Chief Minister of West Bengal and a prominent lawyer, himself appeared for Jayalalithaa and argued that the prison sentence imposed on her had been stayed by the High Court and that her nomination should therefore be accepted. But the Returning Officer, on the basis of the Representation of the People Act and the Election Commission’s 1997 circular, refused to accept Jayalalithaa’s nomination and rejected it.

The editorials written by the *Express* and *The Hindu* on 26 April 2001 about the rejection of Jayalalithaa’s nominations deserve particular attention.

The *Express* wrote: “There is nothing surprising in the rejection of all four nominations filed by AIADMK leader Jayalalithaa. The election authorities have strictly followed the Election Commission’s 1997 circular that a person sentenced to more than two years cannot contest an election. Although the law says that nominations cannot be filed in more than two constituencies, Jayalalithaa tried her luck in four. Jayalalithaa, who does not respect public opinion, calculated that she could come to power by using the sympathy wave that would arise from the rejection of her nomination.”

*The Hindu* wrote: “As expected, Jayalalithaa has been denied permission to contest the election. After the Election Commission affirmed its 1997 order on disqualification, Jayalalithaa’s disqualification became certain. If Jayalalithaa thought that by contesting in four places she might somehow get a chance to contest in at least one, that was a mistaken decision; indeed it became another reason confirming her disqualification.”

On the 14th, even while election results were still coming in, a meeting of AIADMK Legislative Assembly members was convened in great haste. Jayalalithaa was elected leader of the legislature party. That very evening, Governor Fathima Beevi administered the oath of office to her. Newspapers at the time said that the swearing-in was rushed because of fear that someone might file a case seeking to prevent her from taking office.

In its editorial of 15-5-2001, *The Hindu* wrote that this was the first time a person decisively barred from contesting a Legislative Assembly election had become Chief Minister, and also the first time a person legally convicted by a court had occupied such an office; on both counts, it said, the act violated the basic spirit of the Constitution and raised painful questions both legally and in terms of justice.

Some prominent English newspapers also wrote that a person convicted on corruption charges had taken office as Chief Minister and thereby violated moral standards. *Ananda Vikatan*, in its editorial, wrote that Jayalalithaa should sit in the Chief Minister’s chair only after legally wiping away the stain upon her; that alone would be fitting both for the dignity and honour of the office and for her.

When an opinion was expressed that a case might be filed against Governor Fathima Beevi for having administered the oath so hurriedly, Jayalalithaa said that no case could be filed in court against the Governor’s acts and that no one could question the Governor’s decision. Yet it is noteworthy that when Governor Channa Reddy, in 1995, granted permission to prosecute Jayalalithaa in connection with the TANSI corruption case, Jayalalithaa herself filed cases against the Governor in both the High Court and the Supreme Court. Not only that, she also had the Assembly pass a resolution asking the Union Government to recall Governor Channa Reddy immediately.

Some may ask what was wrong in administering the oath to Jayalalithaa as Chief Minister. Her nomination had been cancelled by the authorities on the ground that, as a person convicted of a criminal offence, she was not even qualified to contest the election. Can a person disqualified from contesting an election in that manner be administered the oath of office?

Many corruption cases had been registered against Jayalalithaa. Three corruption cases against her were tried and decided by the Special Court in Chennai. The court held that corruption had been proved and imposed prison sentences. In one case Jayalalithaa was sentenced to one year’s imprisonment and a fine of one thousand rupees. In the second case — the TANSI land-corruption case — she was sentenced to three years’ imprisonment and a fine of ten thousand rupees. In the third corruption case she was sentenced to two years’ imprisonment and a fine of five thousand rupees.

Jayalalithaa appealed against these sentences in the Madras High Court. The Madras High Court stayed only the prison sentences; it did not stay the judgments. Several other cases were also pending.

Lawyers P.R. Kapoor, Dhananjayan Chauhan, Pratap Singh Chautala and P.L. Wadhera filed public-interest petitions in the Supreme Court challenging Jayalalithaa’s assumption of office as Chief Minister. The Supreme Court admitted the matter and ordered that it be heard by a five-judge Constitution Bench.

On 2-8-2001, Supreme Court Judges K.T. Thomas and S.N. Variava, in what the source describes as a striking ruling in a corruption case, stated that until a person convicted in a corruption case is proved innocent by a higher court, that person should not continue in public office; that a higher court should not stay the finding of guilt, though it may temporarily suspend the sentence; and that a person convicted of corruption should not remain in public office until cleared of the charges by a court, nor should a court assist such an appointment.

Citing this ruling, P.R. Kapoor argued before the Supreme Court that Jayalalithaa, described in the source as a person convicted of corruption, should be restrained from continuing as Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu. Following this case, several lawyers and newspapers such as *The Hindu* also objected to Jayalalithaa continuing in office as Chief Minister.

Jayalalithaa had assumed the office of Chief Minister without being an MLA. Within six months she had to contest from some constituency, win and become an MLA. But the TANSI land-corruption appeal was then pending in the Madras High Court. In that situation, she could not contest as a candidate in any constituency. Therefore, the source says, on 13 November 2001 she was forced to leave the office of Chief Minister. In her place, Mr O. Panneerselvam of that party assumed office as Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu.

The people of Tamil Nadu must decide whether a person with such a record has the qualification to find fault with others and conduct demonstrations against them. **(To be continued)**

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**
