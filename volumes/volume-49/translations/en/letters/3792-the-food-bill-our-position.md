**Udanpirappē,**

The Union Government has set out the benefits it says the country will receive from the proposed National Food Security Act. It is said to have been framed on the basis of the Indian Constitution’s principle that people must be given the right to food and their standard of living improved, and on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which recognises every person’s right to the food they need. Tamil Nadu Chief Minister Jayalalithaa, for her part, has set out the disadvantages she says the law will cause Tamil Nadu. Food-security legislation was mentioned in the President’s Address in 2009 and has now come before Parliament for debate. In my statement dated 6 August 2013, I said I would write in detail about the advantages and disadvantages of this Bill and whether Tamil Nadu needs it.

Union Food Minister K.V. Thomas says the Food Security Ordinance was promulgated to ensure food for everyone in India; that its purpose is to provide foodgrains at low prices to eighty-two crore people throughout the country and thereby alleviate poverty; and that it also guarantees supplementary nutrition through anganwadis to children below six years of age, schoolchildren receiving the noon meal and pregnant women.

The Tamil Nadu Government’s principal objections to the Bill are these:

1. the quantity of foodgrains received from the Central pool must not be reduced under any circumstances;
2. confusion will arise because seventy-five per cent of the rural population and fifty per cent of the urban population are to be made eligible for subsidised foodgrains at the national level;
3. since there is no means of producing food in urban areas, coverage there should be one hundred per cent, or at least seventy-five per cent;
4. Tamil Nadu’s present allocation of 2.96 lakh tonnes is expected to fall by at least one lakh tonnes under the Bill;
5. continuing the existing public-distribution system would impose an additional annual expenditure of ₹3,000 crore on the State exchequer; and
6. when foodgrains cannot be supplied, the State Government would have to provide a food-security allowance.

After listing these points, Chief Minister Jayalalithaa stated in her letter to the Prime Minister dated 2 August 2013 that ensuring the food security of States such as Tamil Nadu was the Union Government’s duty; decisions should be taken only after discussion with the States; uninterrupted distribution should be ensured; it should be clarified that the present allocation under the Antyodaya Anna Yojana would not be reduced under any circumstances; and urban coverage should be increased from fifty per cent to the entire urban population.

India has a population of 120 crore, but at present only eighteen crore families benefit through the public-distribution system. Under the Food Security Act, however, fifty per cent of people in urban areas and seventy-five per cent in rural areas—67.5 per cent of the country’s population, or eighty-two crore people—will benefit, according to the Union Government.

Under the law, each person will receive five kilograms of grain every month, with rice at three rupees a kilogram and wheat at two rupees a kilogram. A family of five will therefore receive twenty-five kilograms of rice or wheat.

Although several States have welcomed the Food Security Bill, some, including Tamil Nadu, fear that it will impose an additional financial burden upon them. The financial burden arising from the Union legislation is the subsidy on foodgrains. Under the proposed scheme, the total annual requirement will be 612.3 lakh tonnes. At 2013–14 prices, the subsidy will be approximately ₹1,24,747 crore. Compared with the food subsidy under the existing public-distribution system and other welfare schemes, the additional annual expenditure will be ₹23,800 crore. The Union Government says it will bear this additional cost in full.

Tamil Nadu’s public-distribution system has continued from the period of Congress rule, with some alterations under each succeeding Government. The State now has 33,222 fair-price shops and 1,97,82,593 family cards, of which 1,67,21,538 are rice cards. A further 18,62,615 extremely poor families benefit under the Union Government’s Antyodaya Anna Yojana. Including them, the total number of rice cards is 1,85,84,153.

The Union Government allocates 2,96,000 tonnes of rice to Tamil Nadu every month. Since 2001 it has supplied rice at three rupees a kilogram under the Antyodaya Anna Yojana, at ₹5.65 a kilogram for people living below the poverty line, and at ₹8.60 a kilogram for those above it. At present, the Union Government supplies rice to Tamil Nadu and other States at ₹5.65 a kilogram for people below the poverty line. Under the new law, it will supply the same rice at three rupees a kilogram. Tamil Nadu’s payment to the Union Government will therefore fall by ₹2.65 per kilogram. This is described as a benefit to the State Government under the scheme.

The Bill introduced in the Lok Sabha in December 2011 was followed by comments from State Governments, including Tamil Nadu. Taking account of the Standing Committee’s recommendations and the States’ views, the Ordinance provides Union assistance to meet transport charges, handling costs and the margins paid to shopkeepers when foodgrains are moved to different parts of a State.

On grievance redressal, the law gives State Governments the right to decide whether to create a separate mechanism or continue with the existing arrangement. Section 23 provides Union financial assistance to State Governments to meet the consequences when the quantity of foodgrains allocated to a State from the Central pool is reduced. The law also proposes that the task of identifying beneficiaries should henceforth be entrusted to the State Governments.

Will this Bill affect Tamil Nadu’s existing public-distribution system? That system is at present a joint responsibility of the Union and State Governments. Procurement and storage of foodgrains, and the total allocation to each State, fall within the Union Government’s authority. Distribution within a State, identification of beneficiary families, issue of family cards and supervision of fair-price shops are handled by the State Governments. The new Food Security Act provides for the Union and State Governments to implement necessary reforms in the public-distribution system. It must improve the food-distribution arrangements already operating in each State; it must not disrupt them.

Were the State Governments consulted before the Food Security Ordinance was issued? Chief Minister Jayalalithaa has said that Tamil Nadu’s views were sought in 2011 and that the State sent a detailed reply. The Union Food Minister has also said that discussions were conducted with the States at several stages. It is reported that, after the Parliamentary Standing Committee examined the Bill repeatedly, the Union Government convened a meeting of State Food Ministers and held consultations.

The Union law will benefit 67.5 per cent of the total population. The Tamil Nadu Government argues, however, that because ninety per cent of the State’s population currently benefits under the public-distribution system, the new law may cause harm.

The objections and demands expressed by the Tamil Nadu Chief Minister cannot be dismissed. She too has merely said that certain amendments must be made to the Food Security Bill and listed what they should be. Even at the conclusion of her letter to the Prime Minister on the second, she wrote: “I wish to emphasise firmly that the draft legislation proposed to be placed before Parliament must be amended appropriately and properly in the manner indicated by Tamil Nadu.” She did not express outright opposition or demand that the Food Security Bill be rejected altogether. Yet in her statement, Jayalalithaa has leapt to accuse me of having supported the Union Food Bill and thereby betrayed Tamil Nadu.

The principal Opposition party, the BJP, has stipulated that the Bill must be debated for six hours. Samajwadi Party leader Mulayam Singh has stipulated only that farmers must not be harmed by it. In the view stated by the Communist Party of India (Marxist) on 7 August 2013, five amendments are required: the Bill must be converted into a universal public-distribution system covering everyone in every area; sugar and pulses must be included among the food items; the allocation must be raised from five to seven kilograms per person; foodgrains supplied to the States must not be reduced; and Aadhaar identification and direct cash transfers must not be linked to the public-distribution system. Similarly, the Chief Minister has proposed some amendments on behalf of the AIADMK Government.

The Parliamentary Finance Committee examined the Bill in detail. An AIADMK member served on that Committee. Did he place all his party’s objections before it? When the Committee submitted its report, one other member filed a dissenting note, but no one else appears to have done so.

As far as the DMK is concerned, the Bill must not in any way impair Tamil Nadu’s existing public-distribution system; no one must be adversely affected; agriculture must continue to be developed, the welfare of farmers protected and remunerative prices secured for their produce; and State rights must not be taken away. At the same time, the benefits available under the Bill must not be lost.

The State Government must examine the practical consequences of the Bill in their entirety and state its position. If Jayalalithaa is certain that the Bill will only harm Tamil Nadu and provide no benefit, she can declare that Tamil Nadu will not accept it, just as she declared that the State would not accept foreign investment in retail trade. But the Chief Minister herself has said in her letter that amendments are what is required. All political parties have been pressing for amendments to the Union Government’s Food Security legislation; they have not demanded its withdrawal. That must be kept in mind.

The Union Government must not impose the methods of implementing the Food Security Act upon the States. It must give each State discretion to implement it in accordance with its own conditions. More than sixty years after India gained independence, the emergence of an intention to provide a national right to food and improve people’s standard of living is constructive.

The Union Government must therefore neither expect the Food Security Act to be rejected entirely nor press for it to be accepted unchanged. It must enact it with the urgently necessary amendments and adapt implementation to the systems operating in the States. As far as Tamil Nadu is concerned, the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam will support the Food Security Bill only if it is passed without causing even the slightest injury to the State’s existing rights.

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**

10 August 2013
