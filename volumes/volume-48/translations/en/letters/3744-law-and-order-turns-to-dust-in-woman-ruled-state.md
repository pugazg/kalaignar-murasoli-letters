# 3744. Law and Order Turns to Dust in a State Ruled by a Woman!

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil is reproduced in full below the translation and remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.” Literally evoking “one born alongside me,” Kalaignar uses it as a distinctive address of shared identity, equality, affection, and solidarity within the movement.

**Date:** 29 April 2013

**Udanpirappē,**

When Parliament assembled on 22 April 2013, it had been expected that honourable members would begin the second stage of debate on the Union Budget and subject it to severe criticism. Do you know what was discussed instead? The opposition parties fired question after question about how the report of the Joint Parliamentary Committee on spectrum allocation had been leaked in advance to newspapers and media; about irregularities in coal-block allocation; and about the sexual assault of a five-year-old child in Delhi. The House reached a point where it had to be adjourned.

Television channels that day held heated panel discussions about the sexual assault of the five-year-old girl and about what punishment should be given to those responsible.

The rape of a student on a bus in Delhi last December had itself been raised in Parliament, and the situation escalated to the point where women attempted to lay siege to Rashtrapati Bhavan. According to the Tamil Nadu Police website cited in the source, 484 rape offences were recorded in Tamil Nadu between January and September 2011, whereas 528 were recorded between January and September 2012. Of those 528 offences, the largest number—75 rape cases—were registered in Chennai city alone.

Near Srivaikuntam, a twelve-year-old seventh-standard student named Punitha, who had gone to school, was sexually assaulted by an unidentified man and strangled to death. After learning of this, I sent the district DMK secretary, Thambi N. Periyasamy, to her home to console the girl's mother and provide financial assistance. I also sent DMK Rajya Sabha member and poet Kanimozhi directly to Thoothukudi to console the family. Only after that did the AIADMK government awaken and send a minister from the district to the house with financial assistance.

Not in just one place but across India, and particularly in Tamil Nadu in recent times, reports have continued to appear widely of schoolgirls and poor women being subjected to such cruel sexual violence. The severe punishment for those who commit such acts should be to keep them in solitary confinement for the rest of their lives. The Union and State governments must take a firm decision on this and put an end to sexual offences.

But as far as the Tamil Nadu government is concerned, action in such cases seems to amount only to registering a case at the police station for appearance's sake.

After Chief Minister Jayalalithaa left for Kodanadu on 1 January 2013, a long announcement was issued in her name. It presented a thirteen-point plan to suppress crimes against women. As always, there was praise and glossy publicity—the exaggerated claim that the Chief Minister had made Tamil Nadu a State without rape.

Of the thirteen measures announced by the Chief Minister, the first three were administrative announcements about who would investigate and take action in sexual-violence cases—both future cases and those already pending. The fourth measure was the “Goondas Act,” a law that, the author argues, was already being misused under this government, with people disliked by the authorities being detained under it and then obtaining relief through the courts. Another measure said steps would be taken to establish women's fast-track courts in every district to try sexual-violence cases. Yet another said the government would bear the medical expenses of women affected by sexual violence. What became of all those thirteen announced measures afterwards? Like so many other announcements, did these too remain merely an `ஏட்டுச் சுரைக்காய்`—something existing only on paper?

The present sexual-assault case involving a little girl in Delhi is a cruelty difficult even to contemplate. A man from a neighbouring house allegedly lured the child away with the promise of buying her chocolate, confined her in a room and, together with his friends, sexually assaulted her; the source also describes bite injuries across her body. Hearing her cries from the locked room, people rescued the hungry child. The source states that 48,338 cases involving sexual assault of children were registered in India between 2001 and 2011.

While this case involving the Delhi child was being debated heatedly all the way up to Parliament, what was happening in Tamil Nadu?

On 12 April there was a horrific incident in the industrial city of Tiruppur. An eight-year-old Malayalam-speaking girl, after returning from school and while alone at home, was raped by a group while she screamed.

The girl's mother is Lalitha; her father, Unnikrishnan, works in Chennai. The husband and wife had not been on good terms for some time. The child was studying in the second standard at a private school in Tiruppur. After returning from school, she would normally attend tuition until her mother came back from the factory where she worked. But on 12 April school ended early and tuition was cancelled that day. The child therefore came home. At around the time she was eating, a man named Kannan entered the house. He and three others are alleged to have raped and further abused the child.

When the mother returned home in the evening, she found the child unconscious and carried her to a nearby hospital. After regaining consciousness, the girl described what had happened, and on that basis a complaint was made to the police. The police initially treated the matter casually, according to the source; only after local residents protested strongly did they register a case and arrest all four accused. All four were workers at a local factory. They are also alleged to have threatened that severe consequences would follow if a complaint were made to the police.

The child was receiving treatment at Ganga Hospital in Coimbatore. When Kerala Chief Minister Oommen Chandy enquired, doctors informed him that she was recovering. The Kerala Chief Minister spoke to the girl's family and promised all assistance, including medical help. Moreover, the source says that local Malayali associations were “consoling Jayalalithaa, who was still frozen in shock.”

Meanwhile, on the advice of the Kerala Chief Minister, Kerala Pradesh Congress Committee secretary C. Chandran personally came and enquired after the girl's relatives. He also spoke to administrators of Malayali associations in Tamil Nadu about the matter.

Even after all this, the height of the tragedy is that neither AIADMK ministers from Tamil Nadu, where the assault took place, nor local ruling-party members came forward to see the child, console her mother or provide any assistance. While the Chief Minister of Kerala took steps over the incident, the woman Chief Minister in Tamil Nadu did not issue even a statement about it. Instead, on the 23rd she came to the Assembly with page after page about how law and order was being safeguarded in Tamil Nadu and replied to the debate on the Police Department.

It is not only the Chief Minister. While Tamil Nadu's media and newspapers report and criticise every detail concerning the assault of the child in Delhi, why is there not even the slightest concern about the rape in Tiruppur? Is it because they fear that writing about it or criticising it on television would invite the displeasure of the Chief Minister of Tamil Nadu? When it became known that the victim in Tamil Nadu was a Malayali child, the Chief Minister of that State intervened personally and paid attention. Is it right for the Tamil Nadu Chief Minister to show indifference?

Friend Tiruppur K. Subbarayan, one of the important leaders of the Communist Party of India, has demanded that the Tamil Nadu government take proper action to secure justice for the family of the sexually assaulted student and ensure that the offenders are punished. Rather than Subbarayan making that demand to the Tamil Nadu government, it would be enough if he conveyed it to his party's State Secretary, Comrade Pandian; a way might then be found for the affected child to receive relief.

Nor is Tiruppur the only place. In Theni district, near Andipatti, an SSLC student named Nadiya from Balakombai village was raped and murdered in the first week of this month. Does the government here pay attention to any of this? The Legislative Assembly meets every day—does it at least provide an opportunity to speak about such matters?

When a sexual crime occurs in Delhi, the entire city is in turmoil; processions and siege protests erupt in anger. The moment news comes that a Kerala girl has been sexually assaulted, the Kerala Chief Minister and Malayali associations respond with concern and extend a consoling hand. But in a Tamil State ruled by a woman, are sexual crimes to occur widely with no one even to question them?

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**29 April 2013**
