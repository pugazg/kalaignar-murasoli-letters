# 3780. The Entrance-Examination Verdict — Is Opposition Necessary?

> **Translator’s note**
>
> This translation is intended to carry Kalaignar's voice into clear, contemporary English rather than recast the letter as literary or academic prose. It preserves the source's argument, political directness, rhetorical questions, repetition, irony, factual detail, and paragraph order. Names, dates, figures, quotations, and intentional English expressions are retained. Where Tamil idiom cannot be reproduced literally without sounding unnatural, the English follows its sense and rhetorical force without adding claims absent from the source. The original Tamil is reproduced in full below the translation and remains the authoritative text. `Udanpirappē` is retained in Tamil transliteration rather than flattened into “brother,” “sister,” or “comrade.” Literally evoking “one born alongside me,” Kalaignar uses it as a distinctive address of shared identity, equality, affection, and solidarity within the movement.

**Udanpirappē,**

On 18 July 2013, the Supreme Court struck down the notification issued by the Medical Council of India concerning the conduct of a single, common entrance examination throughout the country for selecting students for the M.B.B.S., B.D.S. and postgraduate medical courses. In its judgment, the Supreme Court pointed out that the Medical Council of India’s notification on the common entrance examination violated the Constitution.

The judgment also stated that admissions already made through the common entrance examination would not be affected in any way. It further held that private medical educational institutions had the right to conduct their own entrance examinations for admitting students.

The Bench consisted of three judges. Chief Justice Altamas Kabir and Justice Vikramajit Singh delivered the majority judgment. The third judge, Justice A. R. Dave, said that he did not agree with the other two judges and that a national eligibility examination was lawful, practicable and necessary for society.

Even so, because two of the three judges who heard the case held that a common entrance examination was unnecessary, their decision has become the final judgment.

The question of conducting a nationwide common entrance examination for admission to the M.B.B.S. course has existed for many years. During the DMK Government itself, a case was filed in the Supreme Court challenging the Medical Council of India’s decision to conduct an entrance examination for M.B.B.S. admissions in every Government and private medical college in India. When the case came up on 18 February 2011, the Union Government’s counsel, Mr Gopal Subramanium, stated that the Union Government had not approved the Medical Council’s notification concerning the common entrance examination. The Medical Council’s counsel, however, said that the Union Government had already granted approval.

Because the Medical Council and the Union Government had taken contradictory positions, the honourable judges hearing the case then advised both sides to sit together, consult one another and arrive at a common decision.

When the case came up again on 7 March 2011, counsel for the Medical Council stated that amended regulations for medical education had been notified on 21 December 2010 and that, under those amended regulations, a common entrance examination for M.B.B.S. admission would be conducted with the Union Government’s prior approval.

The Supreme Court said that the question whether the Medical Council’s amended regulations were legally valid had not arisen in that proceeding. It also said that anyone affected by those amended regulations could challenge them in court.

As far as Tamil Nadu is concerned, the common entrance examination that had been in force was abolished by legislation enacted with the President’s assent. From the academic year 2007–08 onwards, students have been admitted to medical courses on the basis of their Plus Two marks, following the reservation system. When I was Chief Minister, I wrote on 15 August 2010 to the Prime Minister and the Union Health Minister that this practice must continue.

The Tamil Nadu Government also impleaded itself in the case pending before the Supreme Court. It informed the Court that admission to M.B.B.S. courses in Tamil Nadu should continue to be based on marks obtained in the Higher Secondary examination. Meanwhile, on 6 January 2011, the DMK Government also obtained an interim stay from the Madras High Court against the Medical Council’s notification.

At the conference of State Health Ministers held in Hyderabad from 11 to 13 January 2011 under the chairmanship of the Union Health Minister, it was decided that no nationwide common entrance examination would be introduced for medical education. On 13 January 2011, the Union Government directed the Medical Council of India to withdraw the notification it had already issued for a nationwide common entrance examination for M.B.B.S. admissions.

No one will have forgotten, therefore, that it was the DMK Government that abolished the entrance-examination system in Tamil Nadu.

When reports emerged in 2012 that the Union Government was proposing a national entrance examination for medical education, I urged it to intervene immediately and take every step necessary to abolish the entrance-examination system altogether.

Fifteen per cent of the seats from every State are taken into the central pool, and an all-India entrance examination is conducted to fill those seats. Likewise, an all-India entrance examination is held for postgraduate medical courses such as M.D. and M.S. There is no separate reservation at all for Other Backward Classes in the seats taken into the central pool. On 29 September 2012, I asked the United Progressive Alliance Government to intervene immediately, abolish the entrance-examination system in this arrangement, provide for the reservation due to Other Backward Classes and arrive at a just decision.

A total of 115 petitions were filed in the Supreme Court against the Medical Council of India’s notification requiring a uniform nationwide entrance examination for admission to medical courses including M.B.B.S. It is in those proceedings that the Supreme Court has now cancelled the common entrance examination and delivered a historic judgment.

At a time when most people have welcomed this judgment with happiness, Union Health Minister Ghulam Nabi Azad told journalists:

> “We waited in the hope that a good judgment would come from the Supreme Court. Unfortunately, such a judgment has not come. We are somewhat disappointed because we had planned to set many things right. The Supreme Court judgment causes us disappointment. In the present circumstances, therefore, it would be proper to seek a review or pursue some other appeal. I have directed officials to read and study the Supreme Court judgment fully. The advice of legal experts has also been sought. At present, there is no course other than legal action.”

When the question of a nationwide common entrance examination for medical admission had earlier come before the Supreme Court, the Union Government, acting on the recommendation of the Medical Council of India, had taken a position in favour of such an examination.

As soon as I learnt of this, I wrote on 15 August 2010 to Prime Minister Dr Manmohan Singh and Union Health Minister Ghulam Nabi Azad.

I explained that, keeping the welfare of rural students in mind, the Tamil Nadu Government had enacted legislation with the President’s assent abolishing entrance examinations for engineering and medical education from 2007–08; that rural students who were socially and economically disadvantaged had greatly benefited; that an all-India common entrance examination would interfere with the States’ authority to administer education; that it would cause a serious setback to Tamil Nadu’s long-standing sixty-nine per cent reservation; and that the interests of Backward Classes, Most Backward Classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in Tamil Nadu—the homeland of social justice—would be harmed. I asked the Union Government to reconsider the proposal for a common entrance examination and arrive at a sound decision.

Replying on 27 August 2010, Union Health Minister Ghulam Nabi Azad wrote:

> “The proposal to conduct an all-India entrance examination for medical education arises from action taken by the Medical Council of India. The Union Government nevertheless considers it necessary to hold extensive consultations with the State Governments and various organisations and to take their concerns and sentiments into account before arriving at a decision. In view of the concern expressed in Chief Minister Kalaignar’s letter, I assure you through this letter that decisions concerning an all-India entrance examination will be taken only after extensive consultation with State Governments and other concerned bodies.”

This news was reported in detail at the time in all Tamil and English newspapers.

Writing an editorial on the Supreme Court judgment on 20 July 2013, *Dinamani* stated that the Court had ruled that the Medical Council of India possessed legal authority to frame guidelines for medical education, but not to order the conduct of an entrance examination. It observed that the judgment defined the limits of the Council’s authority; it did not say that medical entrance examinations could never be held. Nor had the Court prevented individual States from conducting entrance examinations for admission to medical colleges within their jurisdiction. Chief Justice Altamas Kabir and Justice Vikramajit Singh had expressed the view that a nationwide entrance examination would amount to interference in the rights of State Governments.

The DMK Government abolished entrance examinations precisely to remove the inequality between poor and middle-class rural students who lack facilities and urban students who enjoy far greater advantages.

The Supreme Court’s judgment cancelling these entrance examinations is therefore welcome for three reasons: our policy is that there should be no entrance examination; the all-India entrance examination for medical seats contains no place for the reservation policy; and such an all-India examination intrudes upon the rights of the States.

Union Minister Ghulam Nabi Azad’s announcement that steps may be taken to seek a review of this judgment is not merely unacceptable. I also wish to point out the contradiction between his present announcement and the position he had earlier taken on the entrance-examination question.

As far as the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam is concerned, I wish to make it clear that we shall oppose the entrance examination in whatever form it may appear!

**With affection,**  
**M.K.**  
**23 July 2013**

## Letter-specific notes

1. The source uses several Tamil renderings for the same institution; the English consistently uses **Medical Council of India (MCI)**.
2. The letter concerns a nationwide common medical entrance examination. It does not use the later acronym **NEET**, and the translation does not insert it.
3. **Plus Two** is retained as the familiar Indian term for the Higher Secondary examination.
4. Three punctuation artefacts in the medical-degree abbreviation on PDF pages 133–134 were corrected after visual comparison with the scans.
5. The canonical Tamil source prints the judge’s name as **Vikramajit Singh**. The English now retains that source form rather than silently substituting another spelling.

நகர்ப்புற மாணவர்களுக்கும் இடையே இருக்கும் வேறுபாட்டை

இந்திய நுழைவுத் தேர்வில் இட ஒதுக்கீட்டுக் கொள்கைக்கு
